## Prologue · The Lamp at the Dock

Gleaner's Dock stands at the far eastern edge of the Stele Plain. One step farther out, and you are in the Echolight Reefs.

It is called a dock, but there is no water. Black stone runs all the way to the edge of a cliff, and below the cliff is deep space. In deep space float reef after reef heaped up from old light, like a pod of whales that never slept well, their backs rising and falling in the dark. Half the light bridges that reach out from the Stele Plain start here; half of those who walk out come back here too.

At the end of the dock there is a lamp.

Its base is a column broken off a stele, taller than a man, its surface polished bright by countless hands. Its shade is ground from an old prism; it has cracked three times and been mended three times, and the mends look like three fine wrinkles. What burns in the wick is not fire but broken light — the little shards seekers bring back from deep space and haven't yet taken into the Stele Plain. They melt slowly in the wick and give off a warmish white, the colour of a window on a winter evening.

The people on the dock say this lamp has not gone out in forty years.

That isn't quite true. The lamp went out once, on a night many years ago — only for a moment, as brief as a blink. But of the people who know about that moment, only one is left.

His name is Du.

Du is the dock's lamp keeper. Every evening he climbs the iron ladder beside the column, opens the shade, trims away the blackened wick, sets in new light and closes the shade again. He never speaks while he does it. He is slow, but there is not one wasted step, like a man reciting a prayer he has said thousands of times, every word so familiar it needs no thought.

Once the lamp is lit, he sits for a while on the bench beneath it and looks out over the reefs.

The bench has two seats. He always sits on the left. On the right lies a folded blue-grey blanket, a single character embroidered in one corner. The thread has faded; you have to lean in close to make it out. It reads Shi. The blanket is clean. Every few days it is taken away to be beaten and aired, then folded back exactly as before, the corners turned the same way.

Newcomers on the dock sometimes ask whose seat it is.

Du doesn't answer. He just smooths a corner of the blanket and goes on looking at the reefs, at the old light rising and falling like the backs of whales, and farther still — to the deepest end of the reefs, where a lighthouse flashes every so often. Four times, a pause, then five. Four, a pause, five.

That is the Tidesign Lighthouse. From the dock it looks as small as a star about to go out.

Few people on the Stele Plain want to be a lamp keeper. There is nothing in it to boast about: you don't go out, you don't bring light back, and your name will never be carved on any stele. A lamp keeper does three things — lights the lamp, waits, and writes down the names of those who return. Most nights nothing happens at all. Now and then, deep in the night, a thin thread of light appears out among the reefs, and someone follows it home, walks up under the lamp and kneels on the black stone, gasping. Then the lamp keeper must be the first one to go to them.

Du has done this for forty years. The dock hands have changed many times over, seekers have come and gone, the Observatory's walls have been repainted twice, and he is still here. Some say he is part of the dock, like the lamp column, like the mooring posts, like the hand-polished railing at the cliff's edge — the wind can't blow him away, the tide can't move him. He doesn't argue when he hears it. He just goes on trimming his wick.

The dock has only one rule. It is carved deep into the foot of the lamp column:

"The lamp burns for those who have not yet come home."

So the lamp is never put out. Because out in this deep space, there is always someone who has not yet come home.

## Chapter One · Ting

The day Ting came to the dock, the sky was a grey that had been washed too many times.

The Stele Plain has no true day or night, only the difference between when the core breathes a little brighter and when it breathes a little dimmer. The Observatory calls the bright part day and the dim part night, and everyone simply lives that way. Ting arrived just as day was turning dim. One by one the spires on the Stele Plain lit their lamps, as if someone were walking a very long road, lighting candles one at a time.

She carried a cloth bag on her back and held a letter in her hand. She had held the envelope so long that all four corners had gone soft.

She was twelve. Her mother, Ling, was a seeker. Three months earlier Ling had walked a light bridge into the Echolight Reefs, saying she was going to change the wick at the Tidesign Lighthouse and would be back in two days. Two days later she had not come back, nor two weeks later. People from the Observatory came to the house twice. The first time they said, "Wait a little longer." The second time they said nothing; they only left this letter and told Ting to take it to the dock in the east and find a man called Du.

The dock was quieter than she had imagined. A few dock hands went by pushing empty carts, the wheels making low, hollow sounds on the black stone. Two seekers stood at the cliff's edge tying ropes around their waists, carefully, loop after loop, as if wrapping a gift that must never be opened. No one noticed her.

She walked to the very end of the dock and saw the lamp, the bench beneath it, and the old man sitting on the left side of the bench.

The old man was thin, a little stooped, his hair the grey-white of someone who has stood in the light too many years. He wore a heavy coat washed until its colour was gone, its cuffs frayed. He was polishing a glass shade with a cloth, very slowly: one side, then a look at it against the lamplight, then the other side.

"Are you Du?" Ting asked.

The old man didn't look up. "Depends who's asking."

She held out the letter. He set down the glass shade, wiped his hands on his coat and only then took it. He read it for so long that Ting thought he had fallen asleep. Then he folded it, slipped it into the inside pocket of his coat and patted it once, as if putting something away.

"Ling's daughter," he said. It wasn't a question.

Ting nodded. She wanted to say something. She wanted to ask, "Do you know where my mother is?" and "Will she come back?" and "Why did they send me here?" But the words lined up in her throat in a long queue, none of them willing to go first, and in the end she didn't say a single one.

Du stood up. His knee cracked as he did, and he frowned, as if a little annoyed with it. He went to the right side of the bench, picked up the blue-grey blanket, shook it open and put it around Ting's shoulders.

The blanket had a faint smell of having been aired in the light. It was very warm.

"The dock gets cold at night," Du said. "Sit down. It's time to light the lamp."

He turned and climbed the iron ladder. Ting held the blanket around her and sat down on the right side of the bench. She watched the old man open the shade, trim off the blackened wick, pick a few grains of light out of an iron box with tongs and set them in the wick. As soon as the light touched the wick it melted — first a little blue, then slowly turning a warm white, like someone walking back from very far away, closer and closer, until at last you can see their face.

The lamp came on. Its light fell on her hands, her knees, the blanket.

Far away, at the deepest point of the reefs, a tiny lighthouse flashed. Four times, a pause, five times.

Ting didn't know why, but she suddenly wanted very much to cry. She didn't. She just pulled the blanket a little tighter, looked at that very, very distant lighthouse, and counted along in her head: one, two, three, four. One, two, three, four, five.

Du came down the ladder and sat beside her. Neither of them said anything.

That night Ting fell asleep on the bench. When she woke, there was a heavy coat with frayed cuffs over her, the lamp was still lit, and Du was still sitting on the left, looking out over the reefs, as if he had not moved all night.

## Chapter Two · The Wick

There was more work on the dock than Ting had imagined.

Du got up every day before light. First he went to the cliff's edge to check the stone posts that held the bridges, running his palm down each one in turn; where he found a crack, he marked it with white chalk. Then he went to the storehouse and sorted the broken light left by those who had come back in the night: bright pieces to one side, dim ones to another, and the ones too sharp to hold without cutting your hand wrapped in cloth and set apart. Last of all he came back to the lamp, took down the shade, cleaned it and put it back.

Ting followed him around. On the first day she only watched. On the second, Du pushed a cloth into her hand. On the third, he let her check the posts herself.

"Use your whole palm," Du said. "Fingers lie. Palms don't."

Ting pressed her palm against the cold stone and slid it slowly downward. The stone was rough, worn shiny in places, and in others covered with a thin layer of light like frost. Halfway down, one spot under her palm felt cool in a different way — not colder, but empty, as if a little piece of stone were missing there.

"Here," she said.

Du came over, laid his palm beside hers and held it there a while. Then he took out the white chalk and drew a small cross on the spot.

He didn't say "well done." But that night, when it was time to light the lamp, he handed her the little scissors for trimming the wick.

Trimming the wick takes patience. A burnt wick forms a black crust, and you have to cut along its edge bit by bit without cutting into the layer still glowing underneath. Cut too much and the lamp dims; cut too little and the crust smothers the new light, and the lamp dims all the same. Du stood at the foot of the ladder and watched without a word. Ting's hand shook. When the tip of the scissors touched the wick, it made a tiny sound, like ice cracking.

She trimmed for a long time. When she finished, her forehead was covered in sweat.

"Set in the light," Du said.

She picked a grain of light out of the iron box. It was smaller than her fingernail and warm to the touch, warm as if it had just come out of someone's pocket. She set it in the wick and watched it slowly melt. First blue, then white, then warm white —

Then it dimmed.

Ting's heart skipped a beat. She looked back at Du. He didn't move; he just watched the lamp.

The lamp dimmed for a moment, then came back, a little steadier than before.

"Every grain of light hesitates," Du said. "It's just come back from deep space. It isn't sure yet whether this is a place it can stay. You wait for it."

Ting waited. The lamp burned steadily, its light falling on the dock, on the bench, on the back of the first reef out in the dark.

Down from the ladder, Du showed her a tin cabinet beside the lamp column. In it lay a very thick book with a black leather cover, its edges worn brown.

"The Homecoming Book," Du said.

He opened it. Every page was full of writing, line after line — some neat, some scrawled, some blurred by water stains. Every line recorded the same things: a name, a time, a direction.

"Everyone who goes out from here," Du said, "the lamp keeper writes down when they come back. Their name, what time they came back, which direction they came from."

"What about the ones who don't come back?"

Du didn't answer. He turned the pages back, a long way back to where the paper had yellowed, and stopped at one page. It was only half filled; the bottom half was blank. The last line held a name, with no time after it and no direction.

Ting leaned in to look. The name was Shi.

Du closed the book, put it back in the cabinet and locked it.

"When the wick needs trimming, you trim it," he said. "When the book needs writing in, I write in it."

That night, on the right side of the bench, wrapped in the blue-grey blanket, Ting counted the lighthouse for a long, long time.

## Chapter Three · The Ones Who Return

The first time Ting saw someone come back was on her ninth day at the dock.

It was very late at night. The core was breathing slowly far away, most of the spires on the Stele Plain had gone dark, and only the dock lamp and a few scattered points in the distance remained. Ting had already gone to sleep; Du shook her awake. He said nothing, only handed her her coat and pointed toward the cliff.

Out in the darkness beyond the cliff, a very thin line of light was slowly growing brighter.

It was a light bridge. It stretched toward them from somewhere deep in the reefs. At first it was as thin as a hair, flickering as if it might break at any moment; then it grew thicker and steadier, drawing a gently curving arc through the dark, and the far end of that arc pointed straight at the dock lamp.

"It's looking for the lamp," Du said. "The way home is walked by watching the lamp."

They went to the cliff's edge. A few dock hands had woken up and come too, some carrying blankets, some pushing empty carts, none of them speaking. The bridge drew nearer and nearer, until Ting could see a figure on it, walking very slowly — a step, a pause, another step — like someone who has carried something very heavy through snow for a very long time.

As the figure stepped off the bridge, he almost fell to his knees. Two dock hands rushed forward and caught him.

It was a young man. His face was pale, his lips cracked, and a fine, glowing frost had formed in his hair. In both arms he clutched a net packed with broken light so bright it hurt to look at. His fingers had gone blue with cold, a few of them already unable to straighten, but he held that net so tightly it might have been his own heart.

"You can put it down now," Du said.

The young man looked at him, his eyes a little unfocused, as if he didn't understand the words.

Du stepped forward and laid his hand gently on the net. "You're home," he said, more softly than Ting had ever heard him speak. "You can put it down now."

One by one the young man's fingers loosened. The net dropped to the ground; some of the light spilled out and lay glowing in a small patch on the black stone. He looked at that light for a long time, and then suddenly he was crying. Not loudly — the tears just kept running down to his chin and dripping onto the glowing frost, and wherever they fell, a little of the frost melted.

The dock hands wrapped a blanket around him and helped him to the small hut beside the storehouse. Du crouched down and picked the scattered light back into the net, grain by grain. Ting crouched to help. The light was icy to the touch, as cold as if it had just been dredged from the coldest place in deep space, but after a while in her palm it slowly grew warm.

"This light," Ting asked, "is it his?"

Du stopped.

"Light doesn't belong to anyone," he said. "Light is what broke off the core. It fell into deep space, and someone went out and picked it up and brought it back to the Stele Plain, so every lamp on the plain can burn a little brighter. The light burning in this lamp tonight was brought back by a woman last month. Next month, the light in this net will be burning in someone else's window."

He tied up the net and handed it to the dock hands.

Then he went back to the lamp column, opened the tin cabinet, took out the black book and wrote a line on the newest page. Ting stood behind him and watched him write: a name, a time, a direction — southeast, the Echolight Reefs, near Twin Moon Reef.

He wrote very slowly, pressing hard with every stroke. When he finished, he put down the pen and looked at the line for a while.

"Why do you write it down?" Ting asked.

"Because he came back," Du said. "When someone comes back, someone has to remember that they came back. Otherwise they'll think they're still on the road."

For the rest of that night Ting didn't sleep again. She sat on the bench looking at the reefs and the distant lighthouse, and thought about one thing: if one day her mother came walking back out of that darkness along a light bridge, she wanted to be the one standing at the cliff's edge saying, "You can put it down now."

## Chapter Four · Shi's Seat

Everyone on the dock knew one thing, but nobody said it: Du never sat on the right.

Whether or not anyone else was on the bench, whether or not the wind was blowing, even when he was so tired he had to push on his knees to stand, he sat on the left. Until Ting came, the right-hand seat had held only that blanket.

Now Ting sat on the right every night. The first week she sat very carefully, her back very straight, as if someone might come back at any moment and want the seat. The second week she began to dare to lean back. One night in the third week she leaned back and fell asleep, and her head tipped onto Du's shoulder.

When she woke she jumped and sat up straight at once. Du said nothing. He only moved that shoulder a little, as if it were stiff.

No one came back that night, and the reefs were quiet. The lighthouse shone in the distance: four, pause, five.

"Who was Shi?" Ting finally asked.

She regretted it as soon as she'd asked. Du's hand stopped on the corner of the blanket and stayed there a long time. Ting thought he wouldn't answer, just as he never answered anyone else. She looked down at the toes of her shoes.

"My sister," Du said.

Ting looked up.

"She was six years younger than me," Du said. "When she was little she followed me all over the dock. If I climbed the ladder, she wanted to climb it too. If I trimmed the wick, she wanted to trim it too. She ruined it a few times and the lamp went dim, and she cried, and when she'd finished crying she trimmed it again." His voice was very flat, like someone reading out a very old list. "Later she trimmed it better than I did."

He smoothed the corner of the blanket.

"This blanket was hers. She said the dock gets cold at night and the lamp keeper ought to have a blanket. She knitted two: one blue-grey and one dark green. The dark green one, I burned."

"Why?"

"Because one night I put it over someone who'd come back. The frost on him was so cold the blanket froze too, and in the end it crumbled." He paused. "When she heard, she laughed at me. She said, 'Brother, you can't even hold on to a blanket.'"

Ting didn't know what to say. She pulled the blue-grey blanket a little tighter around herself, as if that might keep something safe for it.

"She went to the Tidesign Lighthouse," Du said.

He said it very fast, as if afraid he might stop.

"That year the lighthouse's light began to go wrong. It used to be four, then five. For a while it became three, four, three, and no one could make sense of it. The Observatory said they'd send someone to look, and she said she'd go. She said a lighthouse is a lamp too, and she knew how to trim a wick."

He looked at the tiny light deep among the reefs.

"The night she left, I happened to be trimming the wick. I was halfway through when she called up to me from the foot of the ladder. 'Brother, I'm going.' I said, 'All right, be careful.' I didn't even turn my head."

The wick burned quietly above them.

"Then the lamp went out," Du said. "Just for a moment. I'd cut too deep, into the layer still glowing underneath. I scrambled to bring it back, and by the time I turned round she was already on the light bridge, a small figure walking away from me, on and on into the reefs."

"She didn't come back?"

Du didn't answer that. He just stood up, his knee cracking again. He patted Ting's head, very lightly, and went off to the storehouse to sort the light for tomorrow.

Ting sat on the bench alone, in Shi's seat, looking at the distant lighthouse.

Four times, a pause, five times.

Suddenly a thought came to her: who trims the wick at the lighthouse?

## Chapter Five · The Voice of the Reefs

On a fine day, the reefs sing.

Ting found this out by herself. That day Du had gone to the Observatory to deliver the book, and she was alone on the dock. She sat at the cliff's edge with her feet dangling over deep space — if Du had seen, he would certainly have hauled her back — closed her eyes and listened.

At first there was nothing but wind. The wind on the Stele Plain is very dry; it blows through the cracks in the black stone with a thin hiss. Then, beneath the wind, she heard another sound. Very low and very long, like someone humming a song on the other side of several walls. It rose and fell, came from the left and then from the right, as if the whole reef were taking turns speaking in different voices.

She opened her eyes and looked at the reefs floating in deep space.

The reefs are piled up from old light. When the first light shattered, some of the pieces were too big to drift far, and they settled here, layer on layer, until they became reefs. They give off a little light of their own, so faint you can hardly see it; but light bounces among them, from this reef to that one and back again, and every time it bounces it makes a small sound.

The Observatory calls this the echolight. To Ting it sounded more like an echo.

When Du came back that evening, Ting told him. She expected him to say she was imagining things, but when he'd heard her out, he nodded.

"Shi could hear it too," he said. "I can't. I can only see it."

"What did she say it was saying?"

Du thought. "She said the reefs were counting."

Ting was taken aback.

"She said that when the light bounces between the reefs, it keeps a rhythm." Du pointed into the distance. "It bounces to Twin Moon Reef and stops for a moment; bounces to the Stardust Gallery and stops; bounces to the lighthouse and stops again. She said if you listen carefully, you'll find the whole reef keeps time with the lighthouse. When the lighthouse flashes four, the reefs echo four; when it flashes five, they echo five."

Ting turned and looked at the lighthouse. Four times, a pause, five.

She closed her eyes and listened.

The wind. Beneath the wind, that very low, very long humming. And then — she heard it. Very soft, very far: one, two, three, four. Pause. One, two, three, four, five. Like someone tapping lightly with a finger on the skin of a very, very large drum.

She opened her eyes, and they were shining.

"It's talking," she said. "The lighthouse is talking to the reefs."

Du looked at her for a long time. That night, when it was time to light the lamp, he didn't let her trim the wick. Instead he had her sit on the bench and tap out the rhythm she had heard on the arm of the bench, beat by beat. She tapped it: four, pause, five; four, pause, five. Du stood on the ladder with one hand on the lamp shade, perfectly still, listening.

On the seventh time through, Ting stopped.

"This rhythm," she said quietly, "I've heard it before."

"Where?"

"In the song my mother sang at bedtime," Ting said. "She counted the beats when she sang. She said she learned it when she was little: count four, stop once, count five. She said if you count like that, you won't get lost."

There was a very soft sound from the ladder. Du's hand had slipped off the lamp shade.

He came slowly down the ladder and sat on the left side of the bench. He looked out at the reefs, and in his eyes was something Ting had never seen before — like someone who has walked in the dark for a very long time and suddenly sees a lamp ahead, but doesn't dare believe it's real.

"That song," he said, "was the one Shi taught the children on the dock."

## Chapter Six · The Signal Keeper

Rovyn came to the dock once a month.

He always arrived before day turned dim, in a dark cloak that reached his ankles, the hood pulled low. He wore a white mask with a crack running from forehead to chin, and beside the crack was a green eye. Only one. The other side was hidden by the mask; you could see nothing there.

The children on the dock were a little afraid of him. When he came walking by, children at play would stop and stand against the wall until he had passed, then carry on quietly. He didn't care, or perhaps he never noticed. He always walked straight to the end of the dock, to the lamp, said a few words to Du, and then stood at the cliff's edge facing the reefs for a very long time.

"What's he doing?" Ting asked.

"Listening," Du said. "He's the one who listens to the signals. Every sound that comes back from deep space, he can hear."

Ting looked at the figure standing at the cliff's edge. His cloak moved a little in the wind, but he himself was perfectly still, like a post driven into the black stone.

She gathered her courage and walked over.

"Hello," she said.

Rovyn turned his head. The green eye fell on her, flickering, very slowly, as if trying to recognise something.

"Ling's daughter," he said. His voice was very flat, without rise or fall, clean as a pane of glass just wiped.

Ting wasn't surprised. Everyone on the dock seemed to know who she was.

"Can you hear my mother?" she asked.

She had practised this question in her head for days. She had thought of many ways to ask — "Do you know where my mother is?", "Is my mother still alive?" — but they were all too heavy, so heavy she was afraid that once they were out she could never take them back. In the end she chose this one, because it sounded most like a question that could be answered.

Rovyn didn't answer right away. He turned back to the reefs.

"The signals in deep space," he said, "are mostly noise from light striking the reefs. Once in a while, inside the noise, there's something different. Very short, very faint, regular. Something like that is usually a person."

"Then my mother —"

"The Tidesign Lighthouse's signal began to change three months ago," Rovyn said. "It used to be very steady — four, five, four, five, like a healthy heart. Three months ago it began to add a beat now and then, or drop one. The Observatory thought the wick was nearly burnt out and sent someone to change it."

Ting held her breath. "The person they sent was my mother."

"Yes."

"And then?"

"And then the lighthouse's signal steadied," Rovyn said. "After she got there, the lighthouse went back to four, five — steadier than before. But she didn't come back."

The wind blew up from beneath the cliff and whipped Ting's hair about. She put up a hand to hold it down. Her hand was shaking.

"What does that mean?"

The green eye turned to her again and flickered once, very slowly.

"It means someone is looking after the lighthouse," Rovyn said. "As for who, I can't tell. Light doesn't speak names."

He took something from inside his cloak and put it in her palm. It was a small, flat stone, warm to the touch, warm as if someone had held it in their hand for a long time.

"This drifted back from the direction of the lighthouse last month," he said. "It drifted to the edge of the Stele Plain, and I picked it up. There's writing on it. I can't read it. Perhaps you can."

Ting looked down at the stone. Carved into it were a few very fine lines, crooked, like a child's drawing. Four short lines, a gap, five short lines.

Four times, a pause, five.

She closed her hand tight around the stone, so tight her palm hurt.

When she looked up, Rovyn was already far away. He walked back along the dock, and as he passed beneath the lamp he stopped and nodded to Du. Du nodded back. Neither of them said a word, yet Ting felt they had said a great deal.

## Chapter Seven · Twin Moon Reef

Du took Ting out to sea in her second month at the dock.

"Out to sea" was what the dock people called it. Really it meant following a short light bridge tied to a stone post out to the nearest reef and walking back the same way. Short bridges like that don't break and you can't get lost on them; the dock people use them to teach children the way. Even so, Du tied two loops of rope around her waist and two around his own, and knotted the two ropes together.

"If you fall," he said, "don't grab the rope. Grab me."

Ting nodded. Her heart was pounding.

The bridge was steadier than she'd expected. When she stepped onto it, the soles of her feet felt something very strange, like standing on a very thick, very soft sheet of glass with bottomless deep space beneath it. She didn't dare look down. Du walked ahead at an even pace, his back steady, like a tree planted in black stone.

The place they were going was called Twin Moon Reef.

Ting had heard the name many times. Half the people in the Homecoming Book had come back from the direction of Twin Moon Reef. But she had never thought about what it looked like.

After about a quarter of an hour, two glowing masses appeared at the far end of the bridge.

They were two very large reefs, one on the left and one on the right, floating in deep space. They were round — so round they didn't look like reefs at all, but like two little moons. One was bluish, one goldish, each turning slowly, in opposite directions. Between them was a narrow gap, and the light in the gap was many times brighter than the light outside, so bright your eyes hurt if you looked too long.

"Every so often," Du said, "they turn until they overlap. At the moment they overlap, the gap closes and the whole reef flashes. Shi said that's the reefs blinking."

They stopped at the edge of the blue moon. Du untied one loop of rope from his waist so Ting could go a few steps farther. The surface of the reef was cold, like frozen stone, with blue light showing through from beneath, blue as a lake in winter.

Ting crouched down, pressed her palm to the reef and slid it slowly along. She was used to doing this by now — on the stone posts, on the lamp column, on every black stone on the dock. With the whole palm. Palms don't lie.

At one spot, she stopped.

There was a carving in the reef. Very fine and very shallow, worn almost invisible by the light. But her palm remembered the feeling — a little piece of stone missing there.

She leaned close to look. It was a few short lines. Four, a gap, five.

Beside the carving was a character. Deeper than the lines, its strokes very neat, as if whoever wrote it had taken great care, carving one stroke at a time.

It was Shi.

Ting turned to call Du over. But Du was already standing behind her. He looked at the character for a long, long time. He didn't crouch down, or reach out to touch it; he just stood there, like a pole that has been blown by the wind for many years and has finally stopped moving.

"She came this way," he said, his voice very hoarse. "On her way to the lighthouse she came this way and stopped here for a while."

"Why would she carve that?"

Du didn't answer. Slowly he crouched down, and at last he reached out and laid his whole palm on the character. His hand was big and rough, the knuckles knobbed, the back marked with several old scars. That hand covered the character and the short lines and lay perfectly still on the blue surface of the reef.

At that very moment, the two moons turned into line.

The gap closed. The whole reef flashed, very bright, bright as day. In that instant Ting saw Du's face — his eyes were closed, and his cheeks were wet.

When the light faded, Du stood and retied the rope.

"Let's go back," he said. "It's time to light the lamp."

Neither of them spoke on the way back. But Ting noticed that Du walked more slowly than he had coming out, and every few steps he looked back. At the two moons, at the deeper places beyond them, at the tiny lighthouse flashing four and five.

## Chapter Eight · The Turning Season

A lighttide was coming.

The dock people knew it from the wind. Before a lighttide, the wind on the Stele Plain changes. It is no longer dry, but carries a little dampness and a little sweetness, like that indescribable smell in the air before rain. The song of the reefs changes too, faster and higher, like a flock of birds crying in panic before a storm.

A lighttide is when the core takes a deep breath. Usually the core breathes shallowly, and broken light drifts out a little at a time; but every so often it draws in a very, very deep breath and lets it all out at once. When it breathes out, the light in deep space surges in like a rising tide, over the reefs and up onto the Stele Plain, shaking every light bridge like spider silk in the wind.

On a lighttide night, no one goes out. Those already out can only find a reef big enough to shelter behind and wait for the tide to ebb before coming back.

Before a lighttide the dock is quieter than usual. Everyone due back has come back; anyone still out won't be coming back in the next few days. The dock hands lined up their empty carts against the storehouse wall like a row of animals waiting to hibernate. Someone from the Observatory came by once, measured the height of the sandbags at the foot of the lamp column, made a few notes in a notebook and hurried off again.

The dock began to prepare. The dock hands moved the broken light in the storehouse to the very back and covered it with thick cloth. Every stone post was checked again, and each had an extra iron band fastened around it. Sandbags were piled at the foot of the lamp column to keep its base from shaking. The Observatory sent over a box of new light, brighter than usual, in a heavy lead case.

"On a lighttide night," Du said, "the lamp has to stay lit the whole time. It can't dim — not for a moment. The ones sheltering out there have to see it, so they know the Stele Plain is still here."

Ting nodded. She carried the lead case to the tin cabinet and set it beside the Homecoming Book.

Those few days, Du coughed badly.

At first it was only a few coughs in the morning, and Ting thought it was the weather. Then it became coughing all day long, coughing until he doubled over, coughing until he had to hold on to the lamp column to stay upright. He wouldn't go to the Observatory to see a doctor; he said the dock couldn't be left. Ting fetched him a thick scarf from the storehouse. He wrapped it on, and coughed just as badly.

"Go and rest," Ting said. "I'll light the lamp."

Du looked at her. There was a little surprise in that look, and something else too. He didn't say yes, and he didn't say no; he just handed her the little scissors for the wick, sat down on the left side of the bench, drew the blue-grey blanket over and laid it across his own legs.

It was the first time Ting had seen him use that blanket.

She climbed the ladder, opened the shade, trimmed the wick and set in the light. Her hand no longer shook; when the tip of the scissors touched the wick, it made a very clean sound, like cutting a fine thread. The light melted — blue first, then white, then warm white — dimmed for a moment, and came back steady.

When she came down, Du had fallen asleep leaning against the back of the bench.

Ting sat down beside him and looked out at the reefs. The wind grew damper and sweeter. The song of the reefs was fast and confused, like a crowd all talking at once, no one able to hear anyone else. But beneath all that noise, Ting could still hear it — four times, a pause, five. The lighthouse was still there. Still flashing, steady, beat by beat.

She took out the little stone Rovyn had given her, held it in her palm and ran her thumb over its carving again and again.

"Are you listening too?" she whispered to the tiny light deep among the reefs. "A lighttide is coming. Is it cold where you are?"

The lighthouse didn't answer. It just kept shining. Four times, a pause, five.

Du coughed in his sleep and pulled the blanket a little tighter.

## Chapter Nine · The Tidesign Lighthouse

The night before the lighttide came, the lighthouse's light changed.

Ting was the first to notice. She was sitting on the bench counting the lighthouse as usual, and halfway through, she stopped.

Four. A pause. Three.

She thought she'd miscounted, rubbed her eyes and counted again. Four, a pause, three. Then four, a pause, five. Then again four, a pause, three.

Wrong. Completely wrong.

She put her fingers on the arm of the bench and tapped along. Four, pause, three; four, pause, five; four, pause, three; four, pause, four. As she tapped, her fingers stopped.

She knew this rhythm.

It wasn't the lighthouse's usual rhythm. It was another song — another one her mother had sung when she was very, very small. Not the bedtime song, but the one her mother hummed the night she had a fever, rocking her by the window. It was very short, only a few lines; she had long since forgotten the tune, but she remembered the beat, because her mother had counted it by tapping her fingers gently on her back.

Four, pause, three. Four, pause, five. Four, pause, three. Four, pause, four.

Her mother had said the song meant: "I am here."

Ting leapt up from the bench and ran to the storehouse. Du was counting the last batch of light, bent over with coughing.

"The lighthouse!" she gasped. "The lighthouse's light has changed!"

Du straightened up. He looked very ill, his lips a little white. He followed her to the cliff's edge and watched the distant lighthouse for a long time.

Four, pause, three. Four, pause, five.

"Before a lighttide, the lighthouse often goes wrong," he said. "The tide shakes the wick."

"It's not wrong!" Ting said. "It's talking. It's saying 'I am here.' It's my mother's song!"

Du said nothing. He turned and went back to the lamp column, opened the tin cabinet and took out the black Homecoming Book. He turned the pages fast, back and back, to where the paper had yellowed, and stopped at the page with Shi's name.

He turned the book over, to the back of that page.

Ting had never seen that side. It wasn't laid out like the Homecoming Book; there were no names, no times, no directions. Instead, in very fine strokes, line after line, were numbers: four, three, four, five, four, three, four, four… all the way to the bottom of the page, the writing growing messier and messier, the last few lines almost illegible.

"This was the night Shi left," Du said. "The lighthouse's light."

His finger moved slowly along the lines of numbers.

"That night the lighthouse flashed this rhythm all night long," he said. "I wrote it down. I couldn't understand it, but I wrote it down. I thought perhaps one day someone would."

Ting looked at the numbers. Four, pause, three. Four, pause, five. Four, pause, three. Four, pause, four.

Exactly the same as tonight.

She looked up at Du. Du looked back at her.

"That song," Du said, very softly, "did Shi teach that one too?"

Ting didn't know. She only knew that the night she had the fever, her mother had held her by the window and tapped on her back, beat by beat. Her mother had said that someone who kept the lamp on the dock a very long time ago had taught it to her. Her mother had said that person told her: if one day you're somewhere very, very dark and can't find your way home, tap out this song with light. Someone will see.

Someone will see.

Far away, the lighthouse was still shining. Four, pause, three. Four, pause, five.

The wind grew damper and sweeter. The lighttide would come tomorrow.

## Chapter Ten · The Night of the Lighttide

The night the lighttide came, Du collapsed.

He collapsed while climbing the iron ladder. The day had just turned dim, the first wave had just surged over the reefs, and the song of the reefs had become a shrill whistling like breaking glass. Du was halfway up when a fit of coughing bent him double; his hands slid off the ladder and he fell backward.

Ting ran to him but didn't catch him. He landed on the sandbags at the foot of the lamp column with a dull thud.

"Du!"

His eyes were open, but he didn't seem to see her. His lips were moving. Ting leaned close and heard him say, "The lamp… the lamp…"

The dock hands came running and carried Du into the hut. Ting wanted to go with him, but she looked back at the lamp.

It was still lit, but it was swaying. The second wave surged up onto the Stele Plain and the whole dock rocked gently; the light in the shade flared and dimmed, flared and dimmed, like a heart beating out of rhythm. The wick hadn't been trimmed. Du had been climbing up to trim it.

Ting climbed the iron ladder.

The ladder was swaying. The wind was strong and carried a cloying sweetness that made it impossible to keep her eyes open. She clung to the ladder with one hand and reached for the shade with the other. The shade was hot, so hot her fingers flinched. She gritted her teeth and tried again, and got it open.

A very thick black crust had formed on the wick. The lighttide's light was too fierce and burned too fast; a crust that usually took a whole night to form had formed in an hour. The layer still glowing underneath was as thin as paper.

She took out the scissors. Her hand was shaking.

Not too deep. Cut too deep and the lamp goes out.

She remembered what Du had said: Every grain of light hesitates. You wait for it.

She held her breath and cut along the edge of the crust, bit by bit. A snip, a pause. A snip, a pause. When the third wave surged in, the ladder lurched and she was thrown against the lamp column; the scissors almost flew from her hand. She held on tight, waited for the shaking to pass, and cut again.

The crust came away. The light underneath was thin and trembling, but still glowing.

She took a new grain of light from the lead case. It was the one the Observatory had sent, brighter than usual and heavy in her hand. She set it in the wick.

The light melted. First blue, a piercing blue; then white; then — it dimmed.

This time it stayed dim for a long time. So long Ting thought it wouldn't come back. All that was left in the shade was a very, very faint glow, like someone very far away holding up a lamp with the last of their strength.

Ting didn't move. She held the ladder, watched that little glow, and counted in her head: one, two, three, four.

A pause.

One, two, three, four, five.

She didn't know why she was counting. Perhaps because she was afraid, perhaps because it was the only thing she knew how to do. She counted, again and again, her lips moving, her voice torn away by the wind so that even she couldn't hear it. One, two, three, four. Pause. One, two, three, four, five.

The lamp came back.

Not all at once, but slowly, little by little, like someone rising from very deep water, nearer and nearer, brighter and brighter. First warm white, then a colour a little brighter than warm white, and finally it settled there, steady — brighter than it had been before the lighttide.

Ting's tears ran down. She didn't wipe them away. She closed the shade, sat at the top of the ladder with one arm around the lamp column, and looked out at the reefs.

The whole reef was glowing. The tide surged in wave after wave; the light between the reefs was a tangle, bright as day, but a sharp, cold brightness with no direction. In that tangle of light Ting saw reef after reef go under and rise again, saw several thin light bridges in the distance broken up by the tide —

And then she saw the lighthouse.

At the very heart of all that tangled light, the lighthouse was still shining. Four, pause, three. Four, pause, five.

Ting reached out and touched the shade beside her.

She didn't know how to tap a song with light. But she knew that over there, they would surely see this lamp.

All that night she didn't come down from the ladder. Every so often she trimmed the wick and set in a grain of light, then sat at the top of the ladder and counted along with the lighthouse. Four times, a pause, five. She counted until her voice was hoarse, until her fingers were blue with cold, until the core's breathing finally slowed, each wave weaker than the last, and the whistling of the reefs faded little by little into a low hum.

Just before dawn, she saw a light bridge.

## Chapter Eleven · The Light That Came Back

The bridge came from the direction of the lighthouse.

It was very thin, as thin as a hair, flickering through the reefs after the tide, winding this way and that as if it might break at any moment. The path it took was strange. It didn't come straight toward the dock, but went around Twin Moon Reef and around the Stardust Gallery, stopping in stretches, turning in stretches. Ting watched it for a while, and suddenly understood: it was following the lighthouse's rhythm. When the lighthouse flashed four, it advanced a stretch; when the lighthouse paused, it paused; when the lighthouse flashed five, it advanced another stretch.

It was using the lighthouse to find its way.

Ting climbed down the ladder, her legs so numb she could barely stand. Holding on to the lamp column, she made her way step by step to the cliff's edge. The dock hands were awake too; some came running with blankets, some pushed empty carts. The door of the hut opened, and Du stood there holding the doorframe, his face as white as paper, his eyes fixed on the light bridge.

The bridge came closer and closer. There was a figure on it.

Ting's heart was pounding so hard it felt as if it would leap out of her throat. She watched the figure, watched it come step by step, very slowly — a step, a pause, another step.

It wasn't her mother.

It was a boy, not many years older than she was. His hair was thick with frost, his lips cracked, and in both arms he clutched a small cloth bundle. As he stepped off the bridge his legs gave way and he pitched forward. Ting rushed to him and held him up with her whole body. He was very cold, cold as a reef just dredged up from the deepest part of deep space.

"You can put it down now," Ting said.

She said it so softly it startled her. It was Du's voice, Du's way of saying it — the words she had heard on this dock for two months and practised in her head for two months.

The boy looked at her, his eyes slowly coming into focus.

"The lamp…" he said. "I came back by watching the lamp."

"I know."

"The one at the lighthouse… told me to watch the lamp." He was gasping. "She said there's a lamp over on the Stele Plain that never goes out. On a lighttide night, it's sure to be lit. She said to count along with the lighthouse, and count until I saw that lamp, and then I'd be home."

Ting's hands began to tremble.

"The one at the lighthouse," she asked, "who is she?"

The boy didn't answer. He untied the small bundle he'd been holding and put it in her hands.

In the bundle was a flat stone. It was very like the one Rovyn had given her, warm to the touch, warm as if someone had held it for a long time. There was writing on it — not lines, but words, the strokes very neat, as if whoever wrote them had taken great care, carving one stroke at a time:

"Ting, you lit the lamp well."

Ting looked at those words for a long, long time.

"She told me to give it to the child who keeps the lamp on the dock," the boy said. "She said the child would be under the lamp. She said on a lighttide night, that child would surely be under the lamp."

"Why didn't she come back herself?" Ting's voice was shaking.

The boy lowered his head.

"The lighthouse wick needs someone to trim it," he said. "All through the lighttide she kept trimming it. She said if she left, the lighthouse would go out, and everyone still out there who hadn't come home would lose their way. She said she'd wait until the next person came to take over from her."

He hesitated.

"She said there was already someone at the lighthouse. That person taught her to trim the wick and to tap out songs with light. That person was very old, and sat in a chair at the top of the lighthouse with a dark green blanket over their legs."

Behind Ting there was a very soft sound.

She turned. At some point Du had come over from the hut and was standing two steps behind her. He had been holding the black Homecoming Book; it had fallen to the ground and lay open — open at the page with Shi's name.

Du didn't pick it up. He was looking at the boy, his lips moving, but no sound came out.

"Dark green," the boy said. "She said she knitted that blanket herself. She said the other one, the blue-grey one, she'd left on the dock."

## Chapter Twelve · The Lamp Keeper

Du was ill for a long time.

After the lighttide passed, he lay in the hut for half a month, coughing a little less each day but never strong enough to get out of bed. The Observatory sent a doctor, who said his lungs had been hurt by the sweet wind of the lighttide and would need slow healing. On the way out, the doctor looked at Ting and said, "The dock is yours now."

Ting nodded.

She got up every day before light. She went to the cliff's edge and ran her whole palm down every stone post, marking any crack with a cross in white chalk. She went to the storehouse and sorted the broken light left by those who had come back in the night: bright to one side, dim to another, the sharp ones wrapped in cloth. She came back to the lamp, took down the shade, cleaned it and put it back.

In the evening she climbed the ladder, trimmed the wick and set in the light. The light melted — blue first, then white, then warm white — dimmed for a moment, and came back steady. She no longer had to hold her breath. She would wait for it.

The boy stayed on the dock for a few days and then went back to the Observatory. Before he left, he asked Ting, "Will you go to the lighthouse?"

Ting thought for a long time.

"Not now," she said. "Someone has to keep the lamp here too."

The boy nodded, as if he understood.

On the evening of the sixteenth day, Du came out of the hut. He walked very slowly, one hand on the wall, the blue-grey blanket around his shoulders. He came to the lamp and sat down on the bench.

On the right.

Ting came down the ladder and stopped short.

Du patted the seat on his left.

Ting sat down on the left. The seat was a little lower than the right one, with a shallow hollow worn into it by one person sitting there for forty years. She settled into that hollow, and it fit exactly.

They looked out at the reefs. The reefs were very quiet. The tide had ebbed completely, and the light between the reefs had gone back to how it had been, dim and slow, humming that low, long song. In the distance, the lighthouse was shining.

Four times, a pause, five.

"The Homecoming Book," Du said. "Did you write in it?"

Ting nodded. She got up, went to the tin cabinet, took out the black book and turned to the newest page. The last line on that page she had written that morning. It was the first time she had written in the Homecoming Book. Her writing wasn't very good yet, the strokes a little crooked, but she had pressed hard with every one: a name, a time, a direction — northwest, the Echolight Reefs, the Tidesign Lighthouse.

Du looked at the line and nodded.

Then he took the book from Ting's hands and turned back. A long way back, to where the paper had yellowed, he stopped at the page with Shi's name. After the name lay forty years of blank space.

He took a pen from his pocket and, in that blank space, wrote a very small line.

Ting leaned in to look.

He hadn't written a time, or a direction. He had written: "At the lighthouse. Keeping the lamp."

When he finished, he put down the pen, closed the book and handed it back to Ting.

"The next page," he said, "is for Ling."

Ting took the book and held it tight against her chest.

The day turned dim. One by one the spires on the Stele Plain lit their lamps, as if someone were walking a very long road, lighting candles one at a time. The dock lamp was lit too, a warmish white, the colour of a window on a winter evening.

Ting stood up and went to the lamp column. She reached out and laid her palm on the shade. It was warm, warm as if someone had held it for a long time.

With her palm, gently, beat by beat, she covered the light and let it go.

Four. A pause. Three.

Four. A pause. Five.

I am here.

A long, long while later, out at the deepest point of the reefs, the tiny lighthouse answered her.

Four. A pause. Three.

Four. A pause. Five.

# Chronicles of the Dive · Volume Two: The Cartographer

## Prologue · The Unfinished Map

In the west wing of the Observatory there is a very long room, and on its wall hangs a very large map.

The map is drawn on a single tanned hide that hangs from ceiling to floor, so wide you have to back away to the far end of the room to see all of it. At the far right is the Stele Plain, black, built up in charcoal layer upon layer; a small lamp-shaped mark on its edge is the dock in the east. Out from the dock are the Echolight Reefs, every reef drawn in fine detail with a line of small writing beside it giving its name and shape. Farther out are the Stele Shadows, a long row of steles whose shadows trail across the map like a line of people with their heads bowed.

Then comes the Mistlight Gorge.

There the map changes. The reefs and the steles are drawn so clearly, but the gorge is only a pale grey wash. In the grey are a few broken lines, a few half-written place names, a few spots scraped away with a knife, redrawn, and scraped away again. At the very centre of the grey is a round patch where nothing is drawn — not even grey, just the hide's own colour, pale, like an eye without a pupil.

Beside that blank, in very small writing, is a single word: Mistheart.

People who come to the Observatory and walk into this room always stop in front of that blank for a moment. Some ask why it was never finished. Some say the person drawing it must have got lazy. Others say it's too deep there, that no one has ever been.

None of that is right.

The man who drew this map is called Wen. He has drawn in this room for thirty years. Every reef, every stele, every bend of every light bridge on the map he put there stroke by stroke. His way of drawing maps is strange: he himself has not gone out for many years. He sits in this room and waits for those who went out to come back, asks them to sit down, listens to them, and then draws.

So this map, in the end, is a map of many, many people's memories laid one on top of another.

He often says a map isn't drawn with the eyes but with the ears. One person says, "There's a sharp reef there," and he draws a point; a second says, "To the left of the sharp reef the light wobbles," and he puts a dot to the left of the point; a tenth says, "That reef is really two, with a crack between them," and he scrapes the point away and redraws it as two. Over thirty years he has scraped and redrawn the reefs so many times the hide there has worn thin; hold it to the light and you can see layer upon layer of old lines underneath, like the rings of a tree.

People at the Observatory say Wen knows better than anyone on the Stele Plain what deep space looks like, even though he hasn't set foot off the plain in years. He only shakes his head at that and says what he knows isn't deep space, but the people who come back.

And the Mistlight Gorge is a place that eats memory.

People who come back from the gorge always come back missing something. One forgets their favourite food, one forgets the colour of the tree outside their door, one forgets why they went out at all. Those who forget a lot can't even describe the way back; those who forget only a little still have a name or two they can never recall, however hard they try.

Wen has listened to stories like these for thirty years, and drawn grey like this for thirty years.

That blank is the one place on which he has never drawn a single stroke.

Some say he is waiting. What for, no one knows. Every day he sits beneath that blank with his back to it, facing the door, as if someone might push it open at any moment and tell him what it looks like there.

## Chapter One · Wen

Wen's eyes had only gone bad in the last year or two.

At first things were just a little hazy, as if seen through a thin sheet of paper. He thought the lamps in the room were too dim and had two more brought in. Then the haze grew thicker. Near things he could still make out; anything farther blurred into a smear. He began using a piece of clear stone ground very thin, holding it in front of his eye to look at the map — look a moment, take it away, rub his eyes, look again.

The Observatory doctor said it was age, and also the light. A lifetime of looking at light tires the eyes.

Wen said nothing more about it. He threaded the clear stone on a thin cord and hung it around his neck, and every day he sat at the long table as usual, waiting for people to come back.

The long table stood right beneath the big map. On it there were always a few sheets of paper, a few sharpened sticks of charcoal, a small pot of ink, a scraping knife and a chipped teacup. The cup always held half a cup of cold tea, because every time he poured it, he forgot to drink it.

Those who came back were brought here. They were usually exhausted; some still had trembling hands, some still had the frost of deep space on them. Wen would have them sit down first, push the half cup of cold tea across and say, "Have a sip first."

They would drink, and pause, because the tea was cold.

Then Wen would begin to ask.

The people at the Observatory all said the way he asked questions was strange. He didn't ask "Where did you go?" or "What happened?" He asked, "When you set out, what was on your left?" He asked, "How long did you walk before you first stopped? When you stopped, what were you standing on?" He asked, "What did it smell like there?"

Some couldn't answer and frowned and asked what that had to do with anything. Then Wen would wait. He was very good at waiting; he could go a long while without saying a word. He waited until they spoke up themselves: "It was like… like a bit of a smell of rust."

And Wen would draw a stroke on the paper.

The young people at the Observatory said privately that Wen didn't draw maps; he drew people. Every little thing a person said — a smell, a sound, a pause — he turned into a line, a dot, a patch of colour on the map. The reefs were drawn in such detail because over thirty years, thousands of people had sat at this table and told him about the reefs.

But the gorge was different.

People who came back from the gorge often sat at this table and couldn't say a word. Not because they didn't want to, but because they couldn't remember. They frowned, opened their mouths, closed them, and in the end said only, "There was mist. A lot of mist."

And all Wen could draw was grey.

Sometimes, on the grey of the gorge, he would draw one very fine line in very light charcoal, and then scrape it away. The young people at the Observatory didn't understand what he was doing, and he didn't explain.

That afternoon he was holding up the clear stone, adding a newly found little reef to the edge of the reefs, when the door opened and the Observatory's duty officer brought someone in.

"Master Wen," the officer said, "back from the gorge. Only her."

Wen put down his charcoal and looked up. The clear stone slid from in front of his eye and swung against his chest.

A young woman stood in the doorway. She was very thin, her hair cut very short, with a thin, scabbed cut across one cheek. She stood very straight, but her hands hung at her sides and her fingers kept moving, as if counting something, or looking for something.

Wen pushed the half cup of cold tea across.

"Sit down first," he said. "Have a sip first."

## Chapter Two · Yi

She said her name was Yi.

Having said it, she stopped for a long time, a frown slowly forming.

"Yi what?" Wen asked.

She opened her mouth and closed it again. Her fingers tapped lightly on the table, a few times, then stopped.

"I don't know," she said.

Wen nodded and wrote "Yi" on the paper, leaving an empty space beside it.

This is what the mist eats most often: names. Not whole names, but the very edge of a name, like a bite taken from the corner of a biscuit. Of every ten people who come back from the gorge, seven remember what they're called but can't recall their family name; remember a friend's face but can't say the friend's name.

Wen had once met an old seeker back from the gorge who remembered keeping a cat, remembered that it liked to sleep on a spire's lamp shade for warmth, but could not for the life of him remember what it was called. He sat at the table a whole afternoon, and when at last he stood up he said, "Never mind. It probably doesn't remember my name either." And he left, and at the door his shoulders shook once.

"Who did you go with?" Wen asked.

Yi's fingers started moving again. Faster this time, as if searching for one key on an invisible keyboard.

"Someone," she said. "A man. We went together. We went in through the Mistgate, him in front and me behind. We had a rope tied between us."

"What was his name?"

Her fingers stopped.

Wen looked at her. Her face showed almost nothing; her eyes stared straight at the table. But her lips were trembling, so slightly you had to look very carefully to see it.

"I don't know," she said. "I know him. I know the way he walks. I know that when he's tired he props his left hand on his hip, I know that when he hums he always gets the same bit wrong. I know he ties ugly knots in the rope and I always retie them. But I don't know his name."

She looked up at Wen.

"How can I not know his name?"

Wen didn't answer. In thirty years he had heard that question at this table many times, and he had never once answered it. Not because he didn't want to, but because there was no answer.

"He didn't come back?" he asked.

"The rope broke," Yi said. "In Whispering Vale. The mist was so thick I couldn't see my own hand. I just felt the rope pulling me — one tug, then another, and then it went slack. I pulled it in, and the other end was empty. The break was very clean, as if something had bitten through it."

She reached into her coat pocket, took out a length of rope and laid it on the table. It was old, the thick hemp rope people who go out tie around their waists, with a knot at one end and the break at the other. The break really was very clean.

The knot was a good one. She had tied it.

Wen noticed that as she laid the rope down, her finger lingered a moment longer at the break, as if feeling whether a wound had healed.

Wen picked up the rope and looked at it beneath the clear stone. He looked very carefully, for a long time.

"The Observatory sent you here," he said, "so I could draw the way you went."

"I know."

"There may be a great deal you can't remember."

"I know."

"What you do remember may make you very sad."

Yi looked at him. Her eyes were very dry, not a tear in them, but that dryness was harder to bear than crying.

"I don't want to draw the way," she said. "I want to find his name."

Wen put the rope back on the table and pushed it toward her. He thought a moment, then picked up the charcoal and wrote two things at the very top of the paper. One was "Yi", with a space beside it; the other was an empty box, with nothing written in it.

"Then we'll start here," he said.

## Chapter Three · The Way to Ask

On the first day, Wen asked her nothing about the mist.

He asked what she'd had for breakfast. Whether the Observatory's bed was hard. Which side of the Stele Plain she'd grown up on, whether there was a spire by her door, and what colour it shone at night. Yi answered each one, briefly, a little impatiently.

"What does this have to do with finding his name?" she asked.

"The mist eats things in order," Wen said. "It eats the edges first, the smallest things, the things you think about least. The edges of names, the edges of paths, the edges of memories. It doesn't swallow you whole. It nibbles, bit by bit, until you don't even know what's missing."

He drew a circle on the paper with the charcoal.

"So to find what's been eaten," he said, "you can't go looking for it directly. You start from what's still there and feel your way outward, ring by ring. When you reach a place that's suddenly empty, that's where it was eaten. Then you wait beside that empty place a while, and sometimes a little of it grows back by itself."

Yi looked at the circle and said nothing.

That night, back in the small room the Observatory had given her, she sat on the edge of the bed and tried Wen's way of thinking for the first time. First she thought of the bed: it was hard. Then outside the window: there was a spire. Then the path below the spire: one of its paving stones was loose and clicked every time you stepped on it. She thought outward ring by ring until she fell asleep. In her dream someone walked ahead of her, counting very quietly.

The next day, Wen began to ask about the Mistgate.

"When you reached the Mistgate," he said, "don't think about him yet. Think about yourself. Your feet. What were your feet standing on?"

Yi closed her eyes.

"A light bridge," she said. "A very narrow one. Walls on both sides. Walls made of mist, very high, so high you couldn't see the top. The walls were white, but not a clean white — a greyish, dirty white, like very old cotton."

Wen drew a stroke.

"Do the walls move?"

"Yes. Very slowly. Like breathing. When they breathe in, the walls draw inward and the path narrows; when they breathe out, the walls ease outward and the path widens a little." She paused. "We timed it to walk when it breathed out."

"Who timed it?"

Yi's fingers stopped on the table.

"He did," she said. "He timed it. He was very good at that. He said the Mistgate breathes in and out in about a count of twelve. He walked in front, counting quietly as he went, and at twelve he'd stop and wait for the walls to ease before going on. I walked behind, listening to him count."

Her voice grew very soft.

"One, two, three… His counting voice was very low, as if he were talking to himself. At seven he always paused, because he said seven is when the mist is thickest and you have to be careful. At twelve he'd say, 'Good.' Every time, he said 'Good.'"

Wen drew very slowly. Between the two grey walls of the Mistgate he drew a fine line, and on the line he put twelve dots, the seventh drawn larger.

"His counting voice," Wen asked, "do you remember it?"

Yi didn't answer. Eyes closed, her lips moved — very, very softly, without a sound, counting. One, two, three, four, five, six —

Seven.

She paused.

Eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve.

"Good," she said.

She opened her eyes. They were red at the rims.

"I remember," she said. "I remember his voice."

Wen nodded and said nothing more. He pushed the paper over to her so she could see the line and the twelve dots.

"This is the Mistgate," he said. "In thirty years, you're the first to tell me the Mistgate has to be counted."

## Chapter Four · Whispering Vale

Whispering Vale was the place Yi least wanted to talk about.

It took her three days to get there. For the first two she kept circling between the Mistgate and the vale. She talked about the path, the walls, when the bridge underfoot widened and when it narrowed. Whenever she came close to entering the vale she stopped, said she was tired and would go on tomorrow. Wen didn't push her. He just nodded, put down the charcoal and pushed the half cup of cold tea across.

On the third day, she began on her own.

"There are voices in the vale," she said.

Wen picked up the charcoal.

"Not the wind," Yi said. "People's voices. Lots of people, very quietly, talking right by your ear. You can't make out what they're saying, only scraps of words, broken up. The Observatory says it's the mist keeping the things people said when they passed through there before, and breathing them out again."

"What did you hear?"

Yi's finger slowly drew circles on the table.

"At first it was other people's words," she said. "'Go left.' 'Watch your step.' 'I'm so cold.' 'I want to go home.' So many of them, like a room full of people all talking in their sleep at once. Then… then I heard my own voice."

"Things you'd said?"

"Things I'd thought," she said. "Whatever I was thinking, it would say first. The moment I thought 'so cold,' someone beside me said 'so cold.' The moment I thought 'how much farther,' someone said 'how much farther' right in my ear. It was copying me. No — not copying. It said it before I did. As if it knew what I was thinking better than I did."

Wen's charcoal stopped on the paper.

"And then?"

"And then I got scared," Yi said. "I was afraid that whatever I thought, it would say out loud. I was afraid that if I thought his name, it would say his name, and then — and then it would take it."

Her finger stopped.

"So I kept not thinking," she said. "I kept telling myself not to think his name. Walking through the vale, I kept counting his numbers in my head — one, two, three, four, up to twelve, and back to the start. I didn't think his name, I didn't think his face, I just counted."

Wen understood what she meant. In thirty years he had heard many people back from Whispering Vale say that the worst thing wasn't the mist or the cold, but having the words in your own heart spoken aloud by someone else before you could say them. Some would never again stay alone in a quiet room; some learned to sing in their heads, loudly, to drown out their own thoughts.

The room was very quiet. The big map hung above them; the grey of the gorge, under the lamplight, looked like a cloud of frozen smoke.

"When did the rope break?" Wen asked.

Yi lowered her head.

"At seven," she said. "I counted to seven, he stopped for a moment up ahead, and I heard him say something. Very quietly. I didn't hear it clearly. Then the rope broke."

"What did he say?"

"I don't know," she said. "I was counting. I was telling myself not to listen, not to think. So I didn't listen."

She looked up at Wen. This time her eyes were not dry.

"The last thing he said to me," she said, "I didn't listen."

Outside, the Stele Plain had grown dark. The Observatory bell rang once, far off and muffled, like a knock deep under water.

Wen put down his charcoal. He reached across the long table and very gently patted the back of her hand. His hand was dry and rough, its fingers black with charcoal.

"Whispering Vale keeps words," he said. "You just told me so."

Yi stared at him.

"What he said," Wen said, "may still be in the vale."

## Chapter Five · Sunken Light Lake

In Yi's third week, Wen's eyes got a little worse.

That morning he sat at the long table, raised the clear stone to the map and looked for a long time, and couldn't make out the line of small writing at the edge of the reefs. He brought the stone closer, then farther, then tried another angle. The writing was still a blur, smeared into a grey worm.

He put down the clear stone, rubbed his eyes and said nothing.

Yi, sitting across from him, saw it. She said nothing either. She just pulled the sheet with the Mistgate and Whispering Vale toward herself, picked up a stick of charcoal and asked, "Where are we talking about today?"

"Sunken Light Lake," Wen said.

That was beyond Whispering Vale. After the rope broke, Yi had walked a long way alone in the mist and come to it. She didn't know how she had got there; she only remembered the mist suddenly thinning, and before her something very large and very flat, like a mirror lying flat in deep space.

"It's a lake," she said. "But there's no water in it. There's light. The light is heavy, so heavy it sinks. Stand at the edge and look down and you can see the light falling layer by layer, down and down until you can't see it any more."

"What's on the surface?"

"Reflections," Yi said. "There are reflections on the surface. But what's reflected isn't the mist, or me — it's other places. I saw the Stele Plain. Upside down, the spires all pointing downward, all their lamps lit. I saw the dock too. That lamp. Upside down, at the bottom of the lake, blinking."

Wen's hand stopped.

"The dock lamp," he said. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure. When I was little I often went to the dock to look at that lamp. The old man who kept it used to give children sweets," Yi said. "In the lake it was upside down, but I knew it. I stood at the edge looking at that upside-down lamp and suddenly I knew the way back. I turned round with my back to the lake and walked away from where the lamp was in the reflection, and I walked a long, long way, and came out of the mist."

Wen didn't pick up the charcoal right away. He sat there as if thinking.

"I can't draw it clearly any more," he said suddenly.

Yi looked up.

"Sunken Light Lake," Wen said. "I want to draw it, but I can't see the paper clearly. I'm afraid I'll draw it crooked, draw it wrong, draw it somewhere else." He put down the charcoal and pushed it toward her. "You draw it."

"I can't draw maps."

"You can," Wen said. "Every word you just said is a map. You just haven't put them on paper yet."

Yi looked at the charcoal for a long time. Then she picked it up and, on the paper, after Whispering Vale, very, very slowly drew a circle.

The circle was crooked, not very round.

"The lake is bigger than this," she said. "Much bigger."

"Then draw it bigger."

She drew another. This time bigger, and rounder. Inside it she drew several lines going downward, layer after layer, for the sinking light. Then in the middle of the circle, very, very small, she drew the shape of an upside-down lamp.

Wen raised the clear stone and leaned very, very close, almost touching the paper, before he could make out the tiny lamp.

He looked for a long time. Then he put down the clear stone and nodded.

"Good," he said.

Yi froze.

"That's what he used to say," she said quietly. "At twelve, he'd say 'Good.'"

Wen didn't reply. He just pushed the half cup of cold tea across, and this time pushed it right up to her hand, very close.

## Chapter Six · The Dock

It was Wen who sent Yi to the dock.

"Everyone who goes out from the Stele Plain," Wen said, "leaves their name somewhere when they set out. The Observatory has a departure list, but half the light bridges out toward the Mistlight Gorge leave from the dock in the east. The dock has a book."

"The Homecoming Book," Yi said. She remembered. When she was little she had seen that black book at the dock; the old lamp keeper wrote very slowly.

"That book only records those who come back," Wen said. "But lamp keepers have a habit: they also note down the ones who set out, somewhere else. Go and ask."

The dock was different from how Yi remembered it, and also the same. The lamp was still there, the column as tall as ever, the bench as old as ever. What was different was that the one sitting under the lamp wasn't the old man who gave children sweets, but a girl in her teens.

The girl sat on the left of the bench with a book open on her knees, cleaning a glass lamp shade with a cloth. On the right of the bench sat a very, very old man wrapped in a blue-grey blanket, his eyes closed, as if asleep.

Yi stood under the lamp a while before she spoke. As a child she had thought the lamp very tall, so tall you had to tip your head back to look at it; now she was grown, and the lamp was still just as tall, only there were a few new notches carved in the column, as if someone were keeping count of something there.

"Are you the lamp keeper?" Yi asked.

The girl looked up. Her eyes were very bright, bright as a wick the moment new light is set in it.

"My name's Ting," she said. "Who are you looking for?"

Yi told her. She told her about the Mistgate, about Whispering Vale, about the rope, about the man whose name she couldn't remember. She told it a bit confusedly, and by the end she herself didn't know what she was saying. Ting listened quietly the whole time without interrupting.

When she'd finished, Ting put down the shade, got up and opened the tin cabinet beside the lamp column. She didn't take out the black Homecoming Book, but another one from the bottom shelf, thinner, with a dark blue cover.

"The Departure Book," Ting said. "Du says that when someone comes back, someone has to remember that they came back; and when someone goes out, someone has to remember that they went. Otherwise, if one day they come back, no one will know who they are."

She opened it. Every line held a name, a time and a direction, just like the book for returns, only all the directions led outward.

"What day did you set out?"

Yi named a day. Ting's finger slid slowly down the page and stopped at one line.

On that line were two names, written side by side and joined by a short stroke, like a rope.

The name on the left was "Yi", followed by one more character. Yi looked at that character for a long time. It was her family name. The character she had written thousands of times in the Observatory's schoolroom as a child, until her hand ached.

The name on the right was two characters.

Yi reached out and touched those two characters with her finger. The ink had long since dried and felt flat. But her finger stayed on those two characters for a long, long time, so long Ting thought she would never move again.

"Qi'an," Yi said.

Her voice was very soft, as if speaking to someone asleep.

"His name is Qi'an."

The very old man on the right of the bench opened his eyes, looked at her once, and slowly closed them again.

Ting gently tore off a corner of that page — just a corner, exactly where the two names and the short stroke were written — folded it, and put it in Yi's hand.

"Du says," Ting said, "a name should be kept close to the heart."

## Chapter Seven · Wen's Wife

By the time Yi got back to the Observatory, it was already dark.

In the long room in the west wing, only one lamp was lit. Wen sat at the long table with his back to the door, facing the big map. He didn't have the clear stone up; he just sat, as if looking at the map, or perhaps not looking at anything.

Yi went over, put the small folded scrap of paper on the table and opened it out.

"Qi'an," she said.

Wen turned, raised the clear stone and leaned close to look at the two characters. When he had seen them he nodded, picked up the charcoal, and at the very top of the first sheet, in the empty box, wrote "Qi'an" stroke by stroke. Then beside "Yi", in the empty space, he wrote her family name.

When he'd finished, he put the charcoal down.

"Found," he said.

Yi looked at the two names written side by side at the top of the paper. She didn't cry. She had thought she would, but she didn't. She only felt that somewhere inside her that had been empty — like a biscuit with a corner bitten off — the corner had come back. It didn't quite fit; its edges were a little ragged; but it had come back.

Outside the window, the Observatory bell rang again, once, twice. The night was deep. Under the lamp, the reefs on the map gleamed faintly, as if there really were water there.

"Master Wen," she said, "why is the Mistheart blank?"

She had wanted to ask for a long time. Every day she sat at this table, and every time she looked up she saw that round, undrawn blank, like an eye without a pupil.

Wen didn't answer at once. He looked at the map, at the blank, for a long time.

"I've been there," he said.

Yi stared.

"Thirty years ago," Wen said. "I wasn't a mapmaker then. I was like you, someone who went out. My wife and I went together. She was better at finding the way than me; she went in front and I went behind. We had a rope tied between us too."

His finger moved slowly across the table, drawing a line.

"We reached the Mistheart. It was very quiet there, quiet like being in a very, very large room with walls on every side that you can't see. The mist in the Mistheart isn't grey, it's white — a very clean white, like snow that has been falling for a long time."

He stopped.

"Then I came back," he said. "Alone."

"And her?"

"I don't know," Wen said. "I don't remember. I don't remember how the rope broke, or what we saw in the Mistheart, or the last thing she said to me. When I came back, the mist had eaten everything about the Mistheart."

He turned to look at Yi. His eyes were very clouded, like two clear stones fogged over.

"It ate her face too," he said. "I remember her name, I remember her voice, I remember the way she walked, I remember that when she laughed she'd lower her head first. But I can't remember what she looked like. Thirty years, and not once."

The room was very quiet. The wick burned softly, making a faint, thin sound.

"So I don't draw the Mistheart," Wen said. "Others have told me about it, many times. I could draw it the way they describe it. But that isn't my Mistheart. Mine has her in it, and I can't remember her. I don't want to put a Mistheart on the map that doesn't have her in it."

He picked up the half cup of cold tea and drank a mouthful. It was the first time Yi had seen him drink it.

"That blank," he said, "is kept for her."

## Chapter Eight · Hands

After that, Wen's eyes got worse quickly.

A week later he could no longer read the writing beneath the clear stone. Two weeks later, looking at the big map, all he could see was a blurred play of light and dark: the Stele Plain was dark, the reefs were bright, the gorge was grey, the Mistheart was a pale patch of white.

He said nothing about it. He still sat at the long table every day, waiting for people to come back.

Only now, it was Yi who held the charcoal.

The ones who came back sat across the table; Wen asked, and Yi drew. Wen's questions were as strange as ever: "When you set out, what was on your left?" "What did it smell like there?" "When you first stopped, who were you thinking of?" Yi drew lines, dots and patches of colour on the paper, and after each stroke she read it out to Wen: "A sharp reef on the left, like a tooth. Two small lights on the right, blinking." Wen listened and nodded, or shook his head and said, "A little more to the left." "That light should be dimmer."

People at the Observatory said the room now had two cartographers. One drew with her eyes, one with his ears.

Yi drew better and better. Her lines were no longer crooked; her circles were round. She learned Wen's marks: a small cross for danger, a small circle for a place to rest, a wavy line for a stretch where the light sways. She also invented a mark of her own: a row of tiny dots with the seventh drawn larger, meaning here you must count as you walk.

Wen felt that row of dots for a long time.

"That's his," he said.

"That's his," Yi said.

One night, when all the returned had gone and only the two of them were left in the room, Yi was sorting the sheets drawn that day, and Wen sat at the other end of the long table, facing the big map he could no longer see clearly.

"Yi," he said suddenly.

"Mm."

"Do you want to go back?"

Yi's hands stopped.

"Back where?"

"Whispering Vale," Wen said. "You said Whispering Vale keeps words. The last thing Qi'an said may still be there."

Yi didn't answer. She looked down at the paper in her hands, at the row of tiny dots.

"I don't know," she said. "I'm scared. I'm scared that if I go in, the mist will eat something else of mine. Eat his name, eat his voice. When it was so hard to get them back."

"I know."

"Why don't you go back?" she asked. "The Mistheart. Why don't you go back and find her?"

Wen gave a small laugh. A very slight, rather bitter laugh.

"I did go back," he said. "In the first year after I came home, I went back three times. Every time, I got as far as Whispering Vale and couldn't go on. I missed her too much. The moment I thought of her, the mist said what I was thinking out loud — so loud I couldn't stay on my feet. Three times, I crawled back."

He laid his hands on the table, palms up. They were very rough, the fingers black with charcoal, the palms crossed with several deep old scars, as if a rope had cut into them.

"After that I stopped going," he said. "I sat here and drew other people's paths. I thought if I drew them clearly enough, then perhaps one day someone braver than me, better at finding the way than me, would walk to the Mistheart for me."

He turned toward Yi. He couldn't see her clearly any more, but his face was turned squarely to her.

"You're better at finding the way than me," he said. "You know the Mistgate has to be counted. You know you mustn't think in Whispering Vale. You know that in the reflection on Sunken Light Lake there's the way home."

Yi's eyes reddened.

"I'm not asking you to go for me," Wen said. "I want you to know that if you do want to go back, you won't be carrying only your own memories. You'll be carrying this map. This map holds thirty years of people."

## Chapter Nine · The Knotted Rope

Yi decided to go back on a quiet morning.

She didn't tell Wen "I've decided." She just came in especially early that morning, spread out every sheet about the Mistlight Gorge on the long table, and laid them in order one by one: the Mistgate, Whispering Vale, Sunken Light Lake. On each sheet, with her own marks, she traced the route again.

When Wen came in and heard the rustle of paper, he knew.

He sat down at the long table and said nothing. After a long while he took something out of the drawer under the table and put it down.

It was a rope. Very long, coiled into a big bundle, the thick hemp rope people who go out use. Every so often along the rope a knot was tied. The knots were different sizes and different shapes: some single knots, some double, some a knot with a small knot tied beside it.

"What's this?" Yi asked.

"My path," Wen said. "The path she and I took thirty years ago from the Mistgate to the Mistheart."

Yi stared. "But you said you don't remember."

"I don't," Wen said. "But my hands do."

He picked up the rope and felt along it with his fingers, knot by knot.

"She taught me back then," he said. "She said the mist can eat what's in your head but it can't eat what's in your hands. So every time we turned, we tied a knot in the rope. A single knot for a left turn, a double knot for a right, and a small knot beside them when we stopped to rest. Wherever we went, we tied. That way, even if we forgot the way, the rope would remember."

His fingers stopped on one knot.

"When I came back, this rope was tied around my waist. The part I'd knotted on the way back, I untied, because that was the way home and I didn't need it any more. But the part from the way out, I never untied. I don't remember what those knots mean. I only know she taught me to tie them. For thirty years I've felt along it once every day."

He pushed the rope toward Yi.

"I can't see any more," he said. "I can feel the knots, but I can't turn them into a map. You can."

Yi looked at the bundle of rope. She reached out, laid her whole palm on it, and slowly felt her way along from the first knot.

Single. Single. Double. Small. Single.

She picked up the charcoal and drew on the paper. A single knot was a left turn, a double a right turn, a small knot a stop. Knot by knot she felt, stroke by stroke she drew. Starting from the Mistgate, inward and inward, through Whispering Vale, around the edge of Sunken Light Lake, and further in —

She reached the last knot on the rope. It was very big, and very messily tied, as if the hands tying it had been shaking.

The line on the paper stopped at one place. That place was exactly at the edge of the blank on the big map.

"This is the Mistheart," Yi said.

Wen nodded.

"The last knot," he said, "she tied. Not me. I remember that shape isn't a knot I'd tie. She tied neater knots than I did, but that one was a mess."

His voice stopped for a moment.

"I've never known what she was thinking when she tied that last knot."

Yi carefully coiled the rope and held it in her arms.

It was very heavy, much heavier than it looked. Thirty years of sweat and charcoal dust had stained the hemp an indescribable deep brown, and some of the knots had been felt so many times their surfaces shone like stones in a river.

"I'll go and see," she said.

## Chapter Ten · The Mistgate Again

On the day Yi set out, Wen saw her off at the dock in the east.

He walked very slowly, one hand on Yi's arm and the other holding a thin cane that tapped on the black stone, tap, tap. The dock lamp was already lit, and beneath it sat Ting and the old man wrapped in the blue-grey blanket.

Ting stood, turned to a new page in the dark blue Departure Book and wrote Yi's name, the time and the direction. When she'd finished, she held out the pen to Wen.

"Cartographer," she said, "would you like to write something too?"

Wen fumbled for the pen and, beside Yi's name, very, very slowly wrote a single character. It was crooked, because he couldn't see the paper.

Ting leaned in to look. The character was "return".

Yi tied the ropes around her waist. Not one but two: one new, her own; the other the old rope full of knots, which she wound around her left wrist, loop after loop, very tight.

"Every time you turn," Wen said, "tie a knot."

"I know."

"In Whispering Vale, don't think of him."

"I know."

"At seven, be careful."

Yi paused. "I know."

She stepped onto the light bridge. It reached out from the dock, into the reefs, through the Stele Shadows, and very, very far away disappeared into a greyish-white mist. She took a few steps and looked back. The dock lamp was behind her, and beneath it stood three people: a girl, an old man wrapped in a blanket, and a cartographer leaning on a cane who couldn't see her.

Wen raised one hand toward her and waved. He waved a little off, a little to the left. But Yi knew it was for her.

She turned and walked on.

The bridge was very long. Crossing the reefs she passed Twin Moon Reef, the two moons, one blue and one gold, turning slowly on her left; she passed the Stardust Gallery, the light underfoot as fine as sand, scattering at each step and slowly gathering again behind her. She crossed the Stele Shadows, the huge steles standing in a row, their shadows falling across the bridge, and she stepped through shadow after shadow, as if stepping past a line of people with bowed heads. Then, at the end of the steles, the mist began. First a wisp, two wisps, as if someone far away had lit a very thin stick of incense; then thicker and thicker, whiter and whiter, until all that was left before her were two high walls.

The Mistgate was just as she remembered. Two white walls, greyish, dirty, like very old cotton, very high, so high you couldn't see the top. The walls were breathing. Drawing in when they breathed in, easing out when they breathed out.

Yi stood at the gate and closed her eyes.

One, two, three, four, five, six —

Seven.

She paused.

Eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve.

"Good," she said softly.

She walked in, and tied the first knot in the new rope.

There were even more voices in Whispering Vale than she remembered. So cold. Go left. I want to go home. How much farther. The voices of thousands upon thousands of people, layered one over another at her ear. She didn't listen, and she didn't think. She only counted — one, two, three, up to twelve, said "Good," and started again. With her fingers she felt the knots of the old rope on her wrist one by one: single, left; double, right; small, stop a moment.

At the deepest part of the vale, she stopped.

This was where the rope had broken. She knew it. Her feet remembered.

She stood there and took a deep breath. Then she did something she hadn't done the last time.

She let herself think of him.

Qi'an.

All at once, every voice in the vale fell silent.

## Chapter Eleven · The Mistheart

The words came from very close by.

Very soft, very low, like someone talking to themselves right beside her ear. It was the voice she remembered — the counting voice, the voice that said "Good" at twelve.

"Yi, I can see it. Ahead it's white."

Just that. When it ended, the thousands of voices in the vale came flooding back — so cold, go left, I want to go home — layer over layer, drowning those words.

Yi stood there and didn't move for a long, long time.

He had seen it. Before the rope broke, he had seen that it was white ahead. He had wanted to tell her. That was the last thing he had said to her, and she had been counting and hadn't listened.

Now she had heard it.

She didn't cry. She put those words into her heart, close to it, together with the scrap of paper bearing the two names. Then, feeling the old rope at her wrist, she walked on.

Single. Double. Small. She went around Sunken Light Lake. The reflections were still on its surface — the upside-down Stele Plain, the upside-down dock, that upside-down lamp, blinking. She didn't stop to look. She knew it was there, and that was enough.

Then the mist turned white.

Not greyish white, but white. A very clean white, like snow that has been falling for a long time. All around was very quiet, quiet like a very, very large room with walls on every side that you can't see. The white swallowed her footsteps, swallowed her breathing. She couldn't even hear herself counting.

The Mistheart.

She felt the last knot on the old rope. That big, messily tied knot.

She stopped and stood in the white.

At first she could see nothing. Then, little by little, things surfaced in the white. Not light, not reefs, but shadows. Very, very faint shadows, as if someone had drawn on white paper with very pale ink, one stroke, then another.

People. The shadows of many, many people. Standing, sitting, some facing each other, some back to back. All very quiet, perfectly still, like a very, very large painting not yet coloured.

Yi walked slowly past them.

She saw a woman holding a child. An old man sitting on the ground with a book open on his knees. A boy with his head tipped back, as if looking at the stars. She saw someone with their back to her, one hand propped on their hip — the left hand —

She stopped.

The figure didn't turn round. Yi didn't go closer. She just stood there and looked at that back, at that left hand on the hip, for a long, long time.

"I heard you," she said quietly. "You said it was white ahead. I heard you."

The figure didn't move. But it seemed to Yi that, very, very slightly, it nodded.

She walked on.

At the very centre of the Mistheart, she saw a woman.

The woman sat in the white with a length of rope tied at her waist, the other end lying on the ground, the break very clean. In her hand she held a knot in the rope, a very big, very messy knot. Her head was bowed, as if she were looking at the knot, or perhaps smiling.

Yi went up to her and crouched down.

The woman raised her head.

It was a very gentle face. Fine lines at the corners of the eyes, a nose that turned up a little, the corners of the mouth curving upward. When she smiled, she lowered her head first, then raised it.

Yi looked at that face and carved it into her heart stroke by stroke. The way Wen had taught her: first remember the edges, the smallest things, the things most easily eaten. The lines at the corners of the eyes. The curve of the tip of the nose. That dip of the head before the smile.

She remembered.

She stood up and tied a knot in her new rope. A very big, very careful, very beautiful knot.

Then she turned and, feeling every knot on both ropes, walked back from the last one.

## Chapter Twelve · The Cartographer

When Yi got back to the dock, it was very late at night.

She came back by watching the lamp. The light bridge reached out of the mist, through the Stele Shadows, through the reefs, drawing a gently curving arc through the dark, and at the far end of that arc was that warmish white lamp. Beneath it stood a girl holding a black book.

"You can put it down now," Ting said.

Yi knelt on the black stone, gasping. She wanted to say something, but couldn't. Ting crouched and put a blanket around her, then turned and, on the newest page of the Homecoming Book, wrote her name, the time and the direction: west, the Mistlight Gorge, the Mistheart.

When she'd finished, Ting put down the pen and said, "The cartographer is waiting for you."

Wen was in the long room in the west wing. He sat at the long table facing the big map, and the half cup of tea on the table was hot. People at the Observatory said that for days now he had poured a cup of hot tea every day, waited for it to go cold, poured it away and poured another.

Yi walked in. Wen turned his head, his face squarely toward her.

"You're back," he said.

"I'm back."

She sat down across from him and drank a mouthful of the tea. It was hot.

Then she told him. About Whispering Vale, about those words, about the Mistheart, about the shadows. About the one with their back to her, left hand on their hip. About the woman sitting in the white, holding a very big, very messy knot.

There she stopped.

Wen's hand on the table had closed into a fist.

"What did she look like?" he asked. His voice was hoarse, so hoarse it was almost inaudible.

Yi reached out and took his hand. Her hand was very cold; his was very hot.

"She had lines at the corners of her eyes," Yi said. "Very fine ones, that deepened when she smiled. Her nose turned up a little, the tip round. The corners of her mouth turned up — even when she wasn't smiling. Before she smiled, she'd lower her head first —"

"— and then raise it," Wen said.

His tears came. They ran from his clouded eyes, down his cracked cheeks, and fell onto the long table, onto the sheet with the Mistgate, Whispering Vale and Sunken Light Lake.

"I saw her," he said. "As you were speaking, I saw her."

They sat there a long time without speaking.

Later, Wen asked Yi to take down the big map. People from the Observatory came to help; they unfastened it from the wall and laid it flat on the long table. Wen felt his way to the blank of the Mistheart and laid his palm on it for a long time.

"You draw it," he said.

Yi picked up the charcoal. She looked at the blank, at that round place like an eye without a pupil, for a long time.

She didn't draw mist. She didn't draw paths. She didn't draw the shadows.

In the very centre of the blank, very, very small, she drew only two dots. Beside one she drew a short line reaching outward, like a length of rope; beside the other, a very big, very messy knot.

Then, beneath the two dots, in very, very small writing, she wrote a line.

Wen couldn't feel the writing. He asked, "What did you write?"

Yi laid his finger on the line and guided it along, stroke by stroke.

"Someone is waiting here."

Wen's finger stopped on the last character and stayed there a long time.

The map was hung back on the wall. The Mistheart is still a pale blank, only now there are two tiny dots at its centre and a line of writing you have to stand very close to see. People who come to the Observatory and walk into this room still stop in front of that blank for a moment. Some ask why it was never finished.

Now there is someone to answer them.

The one who answers is a young woman with her hair cut very short and an old rope full of knots wound around her left wrist. She sits at the long table, across from an old man with a cane, two cups of hot tea between them.

"Because that place," she says, "is kept for the next one."

# Chronicles of the Dive · Volume Three: Before the First Light

## Prologue · The Words on the Wall

The Observatory's oldest wall is at the very back of the great hall.

It is built of black stone, like every stone on the Stele Plain — cold to the touch, yet with a little warmth showing through the cracks. There are no pictures on it, no lamps, only one line of words carved at about the height of a person's eyes:

"Dive into deep space and bring the first light home."

The words are very shallow now. For as many years as the Observatory has stood, that line has been touched. Everyone who has just woken, just been woken, just decided to go out, is brought before this wall to reach out a hand and read it once aloud. So many have read it that where hands have touched, the stone is worn shiny, shiny as a shallow riverbed. You have to look at it with the light at an angle to make out the shape of each stroke.

Few people notice that at the end of that line there is something more.

After the last word, there is a neat square patch of wall a little darker than the stone around it. It is a slab set in later, its edges sealed so smoothly with mortar that the join can hardly be seen. Nothing is carved on it.

People at the Observatory say it was used to patch the wall when the station was built. Others say there were words there once, worn away, so a new slab had to be put in. There are many stories; no one has ever really looked into it. A stone without words isn't something anyone looks at twice.

The Observatory's children like it, though. When the hall is crowded and the grown-ups queue to touch the line of words, the children squeeze in beside it and press their palms to the blank slab to see whose hand is bigger. The slab is very smooth, smoother than the stones around it. It doesn't look as if it has been touched by many people, like the line of words. It looks as if it has been touched by one person, once a day, for a very, very long time.

All except one person.

Every day just before dawn, when the hall is still empty and the lamps aren't yet lit, that person comes from the far end of the corridor. He wears a dark cloak that reaches his ankles, the hood pulled low, and on his face a white mask. The mask has a crack running from forehead to chin, and beside the crack is a green eye.

He stands before the wall and reaches out his hand.

He doesn't touch the line of words. He touches the blank slab. He lays his whole palm against it and keeps it there a long time, as if listening for something.

The people at the Observatory call him Rovyn. He is the one who listens to the signals; every sound that comes back from deep space, he can hear. The noise of light striking reefs, the whispers of thousands in the gorge, the long echoes among the steles, the bursts and booms out by the rifts — he can hear them all and tell them all apart.

But that slab he has listened to for many years, and heard nothing.

The slab is cold, and empty inside. Like someone who said half a sentence and stopped, and never finished it.

He doesn't know why he comes to touch it every day. He only knows that on the earliest day he can remember — the day he walked back to the Stele Plain alone from the direction of the core, half his face burned, remembering nothing — a woman put this mask into his hands, brought him before this wall, and laid his hand on this slab.

She said, "When you can hear it, you'll know what it's saying."

That woman's name was Xi. She was the one who built this Observatory.

She has been gone for many years.

## Chapter One · The Signal Keeper

Rovyn doesn't remember whether he had a name.

Rovyn is the name the Observatory gave him. They said the light in him was the same as the light of the core, so they called him by the core's name. He didn't object. When a man doesn't remember what he's called, whatever others call him, that is what he is.

He remembers very little, and what he remembers is strange. He remembers heat. Terrible heat, heat that made skin curl like paper. He remembers a voice laughing — a woman's voice, a bright laugh, like the first light of morning through a crack in the window. He remembers the beat of a song: four, a pause, five. But he doesn't remember the tune, or the words — only the beat, like a fruit with all the flesh taken away and only the stone left.

He has never told anyone these things.

Sometimes he wakes in the night and finds his fingers tapping lightly on the edge of the bed. Four, a pause, five. He doesn't know why he taps, or who he taps for. He only knows that when he has finished, he feels a little warmer.

His work is listening. Every day he walks the edges of the Stele Plain, stands on the cliffs facing deep space, and stays there a long time. What he hears he writes down word by word in the Observatory's book: today the reefs are quiet; today there are more whispers in the gorge; today there were three great bursts out by the rifts — best not to let anyone go that way.

Once a month he goes to the dock in the east.

The dock has two lamp keepers now. One is very old, wrapped in a blue-grey blanket, sitting on the right of the bench and dozing most of the time. The other is very young, a girl, sitting on the left with a black book open on her knees. The girl's name is Ting.

Rovyn stood at the cliff's edge and listened to the reefs. He heard the dock lamp and the lighthouse at the deepest point of the reefs talking to each other. The lighthouse flashed four, a pause, five; after a while the dock lamp flashed four, a pause, five as well. Back and forth, back and forth, like two people tapping softly on a wall in the dark with a whole sea between them.

He had never heard this before. It was new.

"What are they saying?" Ting came over, stood beside him and asked.

Rovyn thought.

"They're saying 'I am here,'" he said.

Ting laughed, her eyes curving. "You can understand it?"

"I can't understand it," Rovyn said. "But I know."

He didn't understand himself why he knew. He only knew that when that beat fell into his ears, the green light in his chest brightened along with it, once, and again, like another heart counting along.

When he left the dock that day, he didn't go straight back to the Observatory. He went round to the west wing, to the long room.

Wen the cartographer sat at the long table, and across from him was the young woman with the old rope around her wrist, Yi. They were drawing a new map; Wen asked and Yi drew. Rovyn stood in the doorway and listened a while, then looked up at the big map.

On the map, at the very centre of the blank of the Mistheart, there were now two tiny dots.

"Someone is waiting here," Yi said softly, seeing him look.

Rovyn looked at those two dots for a long time.

Without knowing why, he thought of the blank slab on the wall of the great hall.

## Chapter Two · Xi's Notes, I

This is the forty-first day since we came here.

I've decided to start writing things down. Not because there's anything big to record, but because Mu says that if we don't write anything down, the people who come after won't know how we got here. He was gnawing on a piece of hard, dry bread when he said it, crumbs all over him; he didn't look at all like someone saying something important.

But he's right. So I'm writing.

There are twenty-three of us. I can no longer count how long we walked to get here. We set out from somewhere very far away, so far I can't remember what colour the sky was there. We kept walking toward where there was light, because back then we all thought that where there was light, there was a way to live.

The light comes from the core. The closer we came, the more we saw the rings of light around the core, so bright you couldn't look at them. We saw broken light everywhere in deep space too, little points of it, scattered in the cracks of reefs, sunk in the mist, as if someone had strewn a whole morning into the night.

Then we found this plain.

It is black. Very large, very flat, stretching beyond sight. The ground is all black stone, and the stone is cold, but a little warmth shows through the cracks, as if some very, very large creature were sleeping underneath and the warmth of its breath were rising through the seams. Many stones stand on the plain, one after another, shaped like steles. Mu said, let's call this the Stele Plain.

When he said the name, he was crouching beside a stele, tracing the patterns on it with his finger, as if they were a letter written to him.

Nobody objected. We were all too tired, too tired even to think of a name.

There's an old man among us who used to keep a lighthouse. He says his home was by the sea, where there was real water, and real boats on it, and the boats coming back to harbour at night had to watch for the lighthouse to find their way. He says there's no water here, but there's light, and the light is deeper than water. He says that one day this place will need a lighthouse too. Everyone just laughed and said let's get today's tents up first. He didn't take offence. He just walked off alone to the eastern edge of the Stele Plain and stood on the cliff for a long time, as if looking over, on behalf of a lighthouse that didn't yet exist, the place it would shine on.

We made camp at the edge of the Stele Plain. It's very cold at night, so cold we all huddle together, back to back. Mu sleeps next to me. He's been afraid of the cold since he was little, his hands and feet as icy as stones, so when we were small, every night before sleep I'd hold his hands and count a beat for him. Four, a pause, five. As I counted, his hands would warm and he'd fall asleep.

He's taller than me now, and still afraid of the cold. Tonight he reached his hand over again.

"Sis," he said, "count."

I counted. Four, a pause, five.

He laughed and said I count slower than when we were little. I said I'm old. He said I'm not old, I'm just tired. Then he fell asleep, and his hands slowly grew warm.

I couldn't sleep. I lay on the black stone and looked up at deep space. The broken light out there flickered, very far, very small, like a crowd of very, very small lost children.

I was wondering: will anyone go and bring that light back?

Tomorrow we start building the first tower. Mu says he'll build it very tall, so tall it can be seen from the core.

I asked him why he wants the core to see it. He thought and said, "So it knows someone has come."

I told him to put his gloves on first.

## Chapter Three · Behind the Wall

It was Yi who found the box of notes.

That day the Observatory was taking down the big map in the west wing to clean it. It had hung there thirty years; the back of the hide had gathered thick dust and the hanging cords were nearly worn through. The Observatory people and Yi unfastened it from the wall and laid it flat on the long table.

In the wall behind the map there was a square recess.

It wasn't deep, just big enough for a wooden box. The box was very old, the wood split, and on its lid a single character had been carved with a very fine knife: Xi.

When Wen's fingers found the character, they stayed there a long time.

"This room," he said, "was where Xi used to write. After she was gone, the Observatory turned it into the map room. I've drawn here for thirty years and never knew there was anything behind the wall."

Inside the box were stacks of paper tied with thin cord, arranged neatly by date. The paper was very yellow and very brittle; insects had eaten at it in places and water had blurred it in others. The writing was in very fine charcoal, every stroke clear, as if the writer had been afraid the people who came later wouldn't be able to read it.

Yi read the first page. When she'd finished, she set it down gently and looked up at Wen.

"I think," she said, "this should go to Rovyn."

Wen nodded. He didn't ask why.

He only reached out and touched the lid of the box once, touched the character Xi, like greeting someone he hadn't seen in a long time.

Rovyn received the box of notes that evening. Yi brought it to his small room herself, set it on his table, said only "Xi wrote these," and left.

His room was very small: a bed, a table, and a window facing the core. Outside, the core breathed slowly in the distance, a little brighter, a little dimmer.

He opened the box and took out the first stack.

At first he read very slowly. He could see with only one eye; the other was covered by the mask, and the eye that could see was green and always looked through a film of light, so the words wavered slightly in it. He brought the paper close, then farther, found exactly the right distance, and read word by word.

"This is the forty-first day since we came here."

He read on. He read about the twenty-three people. About the black plain. About the name Stele Plain.

When he reached that line, his hand stopped.

"Four, a pause, five."

The green light in his chest flared — so bright the whole small room turned green. He sat there motionless, the hand holding the paper shaking.

Four, a pause, five.

This was the beat he remembered. That fruit with only the stone left. The one thing he had never told anyone, the only thing he was sure belonged to him.

He read on.

"Sis, count."

He put the paper down and pressed his palm to his chest. The light was still beating, fast and erratic, like someone who has walked in the dark for a long time and suddenly hears someone calling him.

He didn't know what that person was calling.

But he knew that person was calling him.

That night the lamp in Rovyn's room stayed lit until dawn. He finished the first stack and read the second, the third. He read faster and faster, as if afraid that if he stopped he would never be able to pick up the next sheet.

## Chapter Four · Xi's Notes, II

The tower is built.

It isn't very tall — much shorter than Mu wanted. We had no good materials, only the black stone of the Stele Plain, stacked one piece on another. At the seventeenth course the stones began to sway and we had to stop. Mu stood on top with his hands on his hips, looking at the distant core for a long time, then turned and shouted down to us, "It's tall enough! You can see it from here!"

Everyone laughed. It was the first time since we came to the Stele Plain that we had all laughed together.

We put a prism on top of the tower. We found it on a reef: transparent, oddly shaped, like a piece of frozen water. Mu said that when light goes into it, it bends. We tried for days, and at last aimed the prism at the nearest cluster of broken light in deep space.

That night, the prism bent the light into a line.

Very thin, thin as a hair, reaching out from the top of the tower into the darkness, all the way to that cluster of light. The line was bright and steady; it didn't sway or break. Mu reached out and touched it.

"It's solid," he said. "You can walk on it."

I wouldn't let him go. He's five years younger than me, and since he was little he's always been the kind of child who has to try everything first. We argued all night, until the whole camp woke and gathered round to watch us quarrel. In the end it was Mu who said, "Sis, someone has to go first. If I don't come back, you'll know this path can't be walked, and no one else will have to risk it."

I said if you don't come back, I'll push this tower over.

He laughed. He said then I'll have to come back, or you'll never manage to push it.

He tied a rope around his waist and tied the other end to the tower. He stepped onto the line of light — a step, a pause, another step. The line wavered slightly under his feet; he held out his arms the way he used to walk along the ridges between the fields when he was little, keeping his balance.

He walked into the darkness. I couldn't see him any more.

I stood at the foot of the tower holding the rope and counted the beat. Four, a pause, five. Again and again. I didn't know what I was counting. I just needed to do something to keep my hands from shaking.

A long, long while later, the rope moved.

Mu came back. There was glowing frost in his hair, his hands were blue with cold, and in his arms he held a ball of light. It was small, only the size of a fist, but very bright, so bright it turned his face white.

He came up to me and put the light into my hands.

"For you," he said.

I held the light, my hands shaking. It was warm, warm as if someone had held it for a long time.

"It's yours," I said. "You brought it back."

Mu shook his head.

"Light doesn't belong to anyone," he said. "It broke off the core. I only went and picked it up."

He turned and looked at the other twenty-two people in the camp. They were all standing there looking at the light in my hands, and in their eyes was something that hadn't been there for a long time.

"Put it in the middle of the camp," Mu said. "Then everyone can keep warm."

We put the light in the middle of the camp and ringed it with black stones. That night the twenty-three of us sat around it and nobody said a word. The light flickered on every face.

At the foot of the tower I carved a line with a knife.

"Dive into deep space and bring the first light home."

When I'd finished, I thought for a while and left a space after it. I didn't know yet what should come next.

## Chapter Five · Nameless Summit

When Rovyn reached the third stack of notes, he found a map.

Xi had drawn it, very simply, just a few lines and a few dots. The Stele Plain, the first tower, then the reefs, then the steles. At the deepest point of the steles, one dot was circled, and beside it were the words "Nameless Summit."

Beside the circle there was another line, smaller still: "Buried here. Until he comes back."

He didn't know who "he" was. He didn't know what was buried, either.

The next morning he didn't go to the great hall to touch the slab. He walked alone to the western side of the Stele Plain, found a light bridge leading to the steles, and stepped onto it.

The people at the Observatory saw him and were astonished. Rovyn rarely went out. He was the one who listened to the signals; his places were the cliff's edge, the great hall, and that small room with only a bed and a window. He hadn't walked a light bridge in many years.

The bridge crossed the reefs and entered the Stele Shadows.

The Stele Shadows are a great line of huge, stele-shaped bodies casting shadows on one another. From the Stele Plain they look like a row of people with bowed heads, standing silently in deep space. Only up close do you see how huge they are: a single stele taller than the whole Observatory, its face covered in deep patterns, as if carved with words that no one can read.

Rovyn walked between the steles. Shadow lay on shadow, dark as the night within the night. He listened all around: between the steles there was a very, very long echo, low, like someone speaking far away — one sentence, a long pause, another.

It grew darker and darker, until all he could see was the green light in his own chest. He remembered Xi's notes saying that the steles were marks left when the first light shattered.

He walked a long way before he reached the deepest point of the steles.

Nameless Summit was a very short stele. Beside the others it was as small as a child — low, rounded, flat on top. Its face had no patterns; it was smooth, as if hands had touched it for many years.

Rovyn crouched, laid his palm against the base of the stele and slowly felt along it.

At one spot he stopped. A little piece of stone was missing there — dug out, and filled back in.

With his fingers he dug the fill out bit by bit. The earth was loose, and he soon reached something. It was a small, flat stone.

He took the stone out and held it in his palm.

The stone was warm, warm as if someone had held it for a long time. Something was carved on it — not words, but a row of very, very fine short lines. Four, a gap, five. Four, a gap, five. Row after row, covering the whole face of the stone.

He turned it over.

On the back only one character was carved. Its strokes were very neat, as if whoever wrote it had taken great care, carving one stroke at a time:

Mu.

Rovyn crouched before that small stele, holding the stone, and didn't move for a long, long time.

He didn't know this character. No — he did. He knew its shape, knew the direction of every stroke, knew that the final dot had been carved especially deep. He had seen it somewhere. Long, long ago, before the heat, before everything had been burned away, a voice laughing very brightly had used this character to call someone.

The green light in his chest shone, beat by beat. Four, a pause, five.

The echoes among the steles suddenly fell silent.

## Chapter Six · Xi's Notes, III

The core's breathing has changed.

In our sixth year on the Stele Plain, everyone felt it. The core used to breathe very regularly, a little brighter, a little dimmer, like someone sound asleep. But this year its breathing has become shallow and ragged. Sometimes it goes a long time without breathing out, and the whole Stele Plain goes dark, dark as the bottom of a well.

There is less broken light in deep space too.

We have seven towers now, forty-odd people, a dozen or so light bridges. Every day people go out and bring light back and put it in the middle of the Stele Plain to keep everyone warm and the lamps on the towers lit. But the light they bring back is less every day. Those who go out come back looking worse every day.

They say the light in deep space seems to be retreating toward the core. The farther out, the dimmer the light; the farther in, the brighter — but the more dangerous.

Last month, someone didn't come back.

He was the first since we came here who didn't come back. His name was Lian, a very quiet young man, very good at tying knots. He went toward the gorge, saying he wanted to see whether there was light inside the mist. He walked into the mist, the rope tugged three times, and then it broke.

We lit a lamp for him at the foot of the tower. I said this lamp must never be put out; let it burn always. If he's lost somewhere and sees it, he'll know the Stele Plain is still here.

That night the camp was very quiet. Some cried; some sat by the lamp and wouldn't go to sleep; someone untied the rope Lian had left, length by length, and retied it, length by length, as if that would bring him back. Mu said nothing all night. He just sat by the lamp, looking toward the gorge.

Mu said, then we need someone whose only job is to watch this lamp.

Later, the old fisherman on the eastern cliff said he would do it. He said that back home he had kept a lighthouse. He moved the lamp to the cliff's edge, as close to deep space as it would go. He said a lamp has to be at the very front, the very outside, so that anyone still out there sees it first.

That is the dock lamp.

Tonight Mu came to find me. He sat beside me and didn't speak for a long time. I knew what he was going to say. I've known him twenty-nine years; I've always known what he was thinking.

"I'm going to the core to look," he said.

I said nothing.

"The light is retreating that way," he said. "There must be a reason. If we don't find out why, one day the whole Stele Plain will go dark and everyone will freeze. Someone has to go and look."

"Someone has to go first," I said. "That's what you said the first time you walked a light bridge."

He laughed. He said, and that time I came back, didn't I?

I said that time it was a ball of broken light. This time it's the core.

He didn't laugh again. He reached over and took my hand. His hand was still icy, icy as stone.

"Sis," he said, "count."

I counted. Four, a pause, five. I counted very, very slowly, so slowly that at last he said, "You're doing that on purpose."

"Yes," I said. "I want you to stay a little longer."

He said nothing, only held my hand a little tighter.

In his palm, with my finger, I tapped, beat by beat. Four, a pause, five. I said, if you can't find your way home, tap this out with light. I'll hear it. However far away, I'll hear it.

## Chapter Seven · The Sevenfold Corona

After Rovyn came back from Nameless Summit, he stood before the wall in the great hall all night.

He set the stone carved with Mu against the blank slab. He pressed it there with his palm for a long time. The slab was still cold, still empty; he heard nothing.

At dawn he went to the dock and found Ting.

"I'm going to the core," he said.

Ting stared. The old man on the right of the bench, Du, opened his eyes.

"Why?" Ting asked.

Rovyn thought for a long time.

"I want to know," he said, "where I came back from."

Du rose slowly from the bench. His legs were bad; he had to hold the lamp column to stand. He went over to Rovyn, raised his head, and looked at that cracked white mask, at the green eye behind it.

"When I was little," Du said, "my grandfather told me something. Many, many years ago, when the Stele Plain was first built, someone walked back from the direction of the core. Alone, half his face burned away, remembering nothing. That night the dock lamp was lit by the woman who built the station, with her own hands. She stood beneath the lamp all night."

Rovyn said nothing.

"My grandfather said that when the woman saw him come back, she didn't cry, didn't call out. She just went to him and said one thing."

"What did she say?"

"You can put it down now," Du said.

Ting turned to look at Du. Du didn't look at her; he only looked at Rovyn.

"Go," Du said. "The lamp will be lit."

Rovyn stepped onto the light bridge leading to the core.

It was a very, very long road. He crossed the reefs, crossed the steles, skirted the edge of the gorge, skirted the rifts that ceaselessly burst open and closed again. The farther in he went, the brighter and hotter the light. His cloak began to smoke, his mask grew hot, and the green light in his chest blazed as if about to catch fire.

Then he saw the Sevenfold Corona.

It was seven rings of light circling the core. One inside the next, each brighter, hotter and faster-turning than the last. Between the rings were narrow gaps, and the light in the gaps was like flowing fire. To pass through the gaps you had to time the turning of the rings exactly; a step early or a step late and you would be burned to ash.

He stood outside the first ring, watching the turning light.

He didn't know how to get through.

The sound of the turning rings was enormous, like a whole sea rolling at his ears. Waves of heat came at him one after another, so strong he could barely keep his feet.

But his feet knew.

His feet were moving. A step, a pause, another step. His body seemed to remember this path, remember the rhythm of every ring, remember when to stop and when to go. He didn't have to think. He just walked — through the first ring, the second, the third.

Between the fourth and fifth rings, he stopped.

He remembered something.

Not all of it. Just a small piece, very, very small, like a flake of ash drifting down from very high up. He remembered that many, many years ago, he had stood here too. Back then he had a whole face, and could see with both eyes. Back then there was no green light in his chest, only an ordinary heart that was afraid of the cold.

Back then, he had counted a beat in his head. Four, a pause, five.

Counting the beat, he walked through the Sevenfold Corona.

## Chapter Eight · Xi's Notes, IV

Mu has been gone a hundred and three days.

Every night I go to the dock. The old fisherman — everyone calls him the lamp keeper now — keeps a seat for me on the bench. We sit beneath the lamp, looking out at deep space, toward the core, and neither of us speaks.

I've learned one thing: to listen.

At first it was by accident. One night I laid my palm on the black stone of the dock and suddenly felt the stone trembling. A very, very slight tremor, one, then another, regular. I jumped, thinking it was an earthquake. But it soon stopped, and after a while it began again.

I pressed my hand down harder and closed my eyes.

It was light. Light in deep space striking reefs, striking steles, striking the edge of the Stele Plain, carried all the way here, into the black stone, into my palm. Every strike was a signal. The signals from the reefs were dense and confused, like a crowd quarrelling; the signals from the steles were long and slow, like someone sighing; the signals from the gorge were faint, so faint I could hardly hear them, like someone talking in their sleep.

I began to listen every night. I learned to tell apart the sounds coming back from every direction. I wrote them down and drew them, sheet after sheet, line after rising and falling line.

I didn't tell anyone what I was looking for. I was afraid that if I said it aloud, I would never hear it.

The lamp keeper says I've gone mad, lying on a stone every night like I'm talking to something under the ground. But for all he says, the next night he kept a seat for me on the bench again, and brought an old blanket too. He'd brought it from home; its edges were worn through. He said the dock is cold at night, and whoever listens to the signals ought to have a blanket.

I'm looking for one sound.

Four, a pause, five.

In a hundred and three days, I haven't heard it once.

The lamp keeper says perhaps he's too far away. The core is so bright and so loud, the light he taps can't get through.

I said he will. He promised me.

Tonight the core's breathing went ragged again. For a long time it didn't breathe out, and the whole Stele Plain was dark as a well; the dock lamp was the only thing still lit. I sat beneath it with my palm on the black stone, listening to the sounds coming back from the core. So loud, so confused, like a whole forest on fire.

And then, beneath all that noise, I heard it.

Very faint, very far, broken.

Four. A pause. Five.

I leapt up and woke the lamp keeper. We lay down together on the black stone, palms to the ground, and listened.

Four, a pause, five. Four, a pause, five.

It was coming closer.

The lamp keeper climbed the lamp column, trimmed the wick perfectly clean and put in a whole box of the brightest light. The lamp blazed like a little sun, so bright half the Stele Plain could see it.

I stood at the cliff's edge, looking toward the core, waiting.

The lamp keeper stood behind me without a word. The wind was strong and made the shade hum. I listened to the beat coming nearer, beat by beat, so near it was as if someone were walking toward me and tapping softly on my chest as he came.

In my hands I held a mask. I had made it every night of those hundred and three days, ground from the whitest stone, very thin and very light. I didn't know why I was making it. I only felt that when he came back, he might need a face.

I don't know whether, when he comes back, he'll still remember me.

I only know I'll be the first one to go to him.

## Chapter Nine · The Corelight Stair

Rovyn passed through the Sevenfold Corona and came to the Corelight Stair.

It was a very, very long stair, rising from the inside of the last ring all the way up toward the core. The stair was made of light; each step was a layer of solidified light, neither hot nor cold underfoot, like stepping into the palm of someone's hand.

He climbed it step by step.

The higher he went, the more he remembered.

He remembered a very large, very cold plain, and twenty-three people on it sleeping huddled together, back to back. He remembered a pair of hands holding his icy ones, tapping in his palm, beat by beat. He remembered a tower that wasn't very tall, with a prism like frozen water on top. He remembered a thin line of light, and holding out his arms the way he used to walk the ridges between the fields as a child, and stepping onto it.

He remembered a voice saying, "If you can't find your way home, tap this out with light."

He stood at the top of the stair.

The core was right in front of him.

All around him it suddenly went very quiet. The roar of the Sevenfold Corona was left behind him, like a door closing gently at his back.

It was smaller than he had imagined. Not a sun, not a ball of fire, but something very quiet hanging in the middle of the dark. It had no shape and no edges, like a thought that hadn't yet decided what to become. Down its very middle ran a crack, a thin one from top to bottom, like a closed eye.

The core was breathing.

When it breathed in, the crack drew slightly tighter and the light around it dimmed a little. When it breathed out, the crack eased slightly open, and a very, very faint wisp of light drifted out through it, toward deep space.

Rovyn watched that wisp of light drift out — past the Sevenfold Corona, past the rifts, past the gorge, past the steles, past the reefs. He knew where it would drift. It would settle in the crack of some reef, deep in some mist, in the shadow of some stele, and sleep there quietly, waiting for the day someone came along a light bridge, picked it up, carried it back to the Stele Plain and put it in some lamp.

And then that lamp would shine. On a girl who keeps a lamp, on an old man who draws maps, on a young woman with a rope around her wrist. On every window on the Stele Plain.

And then one day the light in that lamp would burn out and become a very, very faint warmth, spreading into the air, into the cracks in the black stone, sinking little by little back into the ground, back into the core.

The core drew a breath.

He understood.

The light wasn't retreating. The light was only travelling a very, very long road. Out from the core, picked up and brought back, shared among everyone, burned away, and back to the core again. Round and round, like breathing.

Many, many years ago he had stood here and seen the same thing. Back then he was too young, too hasty. He thought the light was retreating, thought the core was dying. He wanted to force the crack open, to let more light rush out all at once. He reached out his hand —

And then came the heat. Terrible heat.

He looked down at his hand. On the palm was a very, very old burn, shaped like a closed eye.

He didn't reach out again.

He only stood before the core, closed his eyes, and listened to it breathe. In, out. In, out.

Beneath the core's breathing, he heard a very faint, regular sound.

Four, a pause, five.

It was coming from the direction of the Stele Plain.

## Chapter Ten · Xi's Notes, V

He came back.

He walked back alone. He walked very slowly, a step, a pause, another step. Most of his cloak had burned away, and his face —

I won't write about his face.

As he stepped off the light bridge he almost fell to his knees. I went to him and held him up. He was very hot, hot as a stone just taken from a fire. One of his eyes was closed and the other open, and the open one was green, green as the light at the deepest heart of the core.

In that moment I remembered him as a child walking a field ridge for the first time, arms out, wobbling, turning halfway to look back at me, as if making sure I was still there.

He looked at me.

He didn't know me.

I could see it. His eyes were empty, like a window blown open by the wind with nothing inside. He looked at my face as if looking at a stranger.

I didn't cry. I had counted the beat for a hundred and three days; I knew I wouldn't cry.

I said, "You can put it down now."

He paused. He wasn't holding anything, but slowly his fingers loosened, as if he really had put down something very, very heavy.

I gave him the mask. He looked at the white mask for a long time, and then put it on. It covered exactly the burned half of his face, leaving only the green eye showing.

"What is my name?" he asked.

His voice had changed. It had become very flat, without rise or fall, clean as a pane of glass just wiped. But beneath that flatness I could still hear him. The one who stood on top of the tower with his hands on his hips shouting "It's tall enough!", the one who gnawed bread until he was covered in crumbs, the one who reached out his hand and said, "Sis, count."

I wanted to tell him. I wanted to say, your name is Mu. You're my brother. You've been afraid of the cold since you were little.

But I didn't.

I looked at that green eye, at that empty window with nothing in it. I thought: if I tell him now, he'll believe me. He'll nod and say "All right," and call me sister. But that would only be a name I gave him, not one he found for himself.

I want him to find it himself. Or, if he can't, then let him live a good life under a new name.

"The people at the Observatory," I said, "will call you Rovyn. Because the light in you is the same as the core's."

He nodded. "All right."

The way he said "All right" was exactly the same as when he was little.

I turned away and pretended to adjust the lamp shade so he wouldn't see my face. The lamp keeper coughed beside us and put an old blanket around his shoulders.

That night I went back to the Observatory, to the wall in the great hall. The line of words was still there: "Dive into deep space and bring the first light home." The space after it I had left for six years, never knowing what to write.

Now I knew.

I carved it. When I'd finished, I found a stone slab and sealed that part away. Sealed it smooth, so smooth no one could tell.

I don't want others to read it. I want him to read it. One day, when he walks up to that wall himself and reaches out his hand and can hear what that stone is saying.

The next morning I took him to the wall. I laid his hand on the slab.

"When you can hear it," I said, "you'll know what it's saying."

This is the last page I will write. I'm old; my hands have begun to shake, and I can't write well any more. I'm putting these pages into a box, and the box into the wall.

Mu, if you've read this far —

Count.

## Chapter Eleven · The Way Home

When Rovyn walked back down from the Corelight Stair, he didn't look back.

He went down the stair, step by step. Through the Sevenfold Corona, ring by ring. He didn't have to think; his feet remembered the rhythm of every ring, when to stop and when to go. He walked steadily, more steadily than on the way in.

The green light in his chest shone quietly. It no longer beat, but shone steadily, like a lamp with a freshly changed wick.

He passed the rifts. The cracks burst open and closed on his left and right, burst and closed, light spraying out and swallowed back. He listened to those bursts, and for the first time they didn't sound like explosions to him, but like a very, very large, clumsy kind of breathing.

He skirted the edge of the gorge. In the mist were the whispers of thousands upon thousands — so cold, go left, I want to go home. At the very bottom of those voices he heard two very quiet voices, as if asleep. One was counting, up to twelve, then saying "Good"; one was laughing, and before laughing, lowered its head.

He passed the steles. One huge stele after another, heads bowed, in a long line. As he passed Nameless Summit he stopped, laid his palm on that small stele, and kept it there a while.

"I've read it," he said.

The stele didn't answer. But the very, very long echo between the steles seemed, in that moment, to grow a little lighter.

He entered the reefs.

The song of the reefs rose to meet him, fast and confused, like a crowd quarrelling. Beneath all that noise, he heard it.

Four, a pause, five.

It was the lighthouse. The tiny lighthouse at the deepest point of the reefs, flashing, beat by beat. As he passed it he looked up and saw two figures at the top. One very, very old, a dark green blanket over their legs; one younger, bent over, trimming the wick.

He remembered the old man in Xi's notes, the one who stood on the cliff for so long, looking over, on behalf of a lighthouse that didn't yet exist, the place it would shine on. Now the lighthouse was there, and there were people on it, and people on the dock too. Between the two lamps lay a whole sea that sang.

The younger one looked up, saw him, and waved.

Rovyn waved back.

He walked on.

Then he saw the dock lamp.

Very far, very small, a warmish white, the colour of a window on a winter evening. It flashed in the dark, beat by beat: four, a pause, five.

Someone stood beneath it.

He came closer and closer, close enough to see the person's face. It was a girl in her teens, her hair tangled by the wind, a black book in her arms. She stood at the very edge of the cliff, perfectly still, watching him come home.

He stepped off the light bridge.

He didn't fall to his knees. He stood steady.

The girl came to him. She didn't hold him up, because he didn't need it. She only stood before him, raised her head, and looked at that cracked white mask, at the green eye behind it.

"You can put it down now," Ting said.

Rovyn stood there and didn't move for a long, long time.

Then slowly he raised his hand to the side of the mask. His fingers touched its edge, paused, and fell away again.

He didn't take off the mask. He didn't need to yet.

"All right," he said.

## Chapter Twelve · The Other Half of the Wall

When Rovyn got back to the Observatory, it was not yet light.

The great hall was empty, its lamps not yet lit. He walked down the corridor, step by step, to the very back, to the wall.

The line of words was still there. Touched by countless hands, worn very, very shallow, readable only with the light at an angle: "Dive into deep space and bring the first light home."

He reached out and laid his palm on the blank slab.

The slab was cold.

He closed his eyes.

He no longer listened to the slab. He listened to himself. To the green light in his chest, shining quietly. To the place where his palm met the stone, where a little warmth was seeping slowly from his hand into the stone.

Then with his finger, on the slab, he tapped, beat by beat.

Four. A pause. Five.

The slab moved.

A very, very faint sound, like ice cracking. The mortar sealing the slab's edges split in a fine line. The line grew wider and wider, and at last the whole slab came loose from the wall and fell into his hands.

Behind the slab, there were words.

Carved very deep, very neat, as if whoever wrote them had taken great care, carving one stroke at a time. They followed on from the old line exactly, as if that line had never been broken:

"— and give it to those still waiting."

Rovyn looked at those words.

Dive into deep space, bring the first light home, and give it to those still waiting.

Beneath them was another line, smaller still. So small you had to lean very, very close to read it:

"Mu, come home."

He stood there. The great hall was very quiet, so quiet he could hear the distant core breathing. In, out. In, out. Outside the window, day was beginning to break. One by one the spires on the Stele Plain woke from the night, like a crowd of people slowly opening their eyes.

He didn't cry. He remembered that he hadn't cried in many, many years.

Nor did he remember everything. What he remembered were only very, very small things. A pair of hands holding his. A voice counting a beat in the dark. "Sis, count." A name.

He didn't know whether he would remember more. Perhaps he would, perhaps not.

But none of that mattered any more.

That evening, Rovyn went to the dock.

The dock lamp was already lit. Two people sat on the bench: Ting on the left, Du on the right, the blue-grey blanket over Du's legs. Rovyn went over and sat down on the black stone beside the bench. He had never sat down on the dock before.

Ting glanced at him, said nothing, and shifted over a little, leaving a small space on the bench.

Rovyn sat in it.

The three of them sat beneath the lamp, looking out at the reefs. The reefs were very quiet, humming that low, long song. In the distance, the lighthouse was shining.

Four, a pause, five.

The dock lamp answered it. Four, a pause, five.

Rovyn listened. He is the one who listens to the signals; every sound in deep space, he can hear. But this was the first time he had heard a signal meant for him.

I am here.

You are here too.

Welcome home.

"Ting," he said.

"Mm."

"My name is Mu."

Ting turned to look at him. Du opened his eyes as well.

"But keep calling me Rovyn," he said. "I like this name too."

Ting laughed, her eyes curving.

"All right," she said.

The lamp burned quietly above them, a warmish white, the colour of a window on a winter evening. Below, the reefs hummed their song. Very, very far away, the core breathed slowly.

On the Stele Plain, every lamp was lit.
